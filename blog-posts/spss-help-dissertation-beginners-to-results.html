SPSS Help for Dissertations: What to Run, How to Report, When to Get Help

Got survey data and no idea what to click in SPSS? This beginner-to-results guide shows exactly which test to run, the menu path to find it, how to read alpha, R2, VIF and p-values, and how to report everything in APA 7.

If you are searching for SPSS help dissertation support, you are not alone. Most quantitative dissertation students do not struggle with writing — they struggle with the gap between a Qualtrics or Google Forms download and a defensible Chapter 4. Which test answers your research questions? Where is it hidden in the Analyze menu? What does that wall of SPSS output actually mean? And how do you turn it into APA tables your supervisor will accept?

This guide answers all four. It is written for quant beginners who have survey data, Likert scales, and a deadline — and who want to know what tests to run in SPSS for a survey, how to run them correctly, and when paying for help saves more time and marks than it costs.

Rule of thumb: your research questions choose the test, not the other way round. If you can state each hypothesis as “X relates to / predicts / differs by Y”, you can pick the right SPSS procedure in under five minutes with the table below.

1. Which Test When: The Dissertation Decision Table

Almost every undergraduate and Masters survey dissertation uses the same six procedures. Match your question wording and variable types to the first column:

What do respondents look like? (age, gender, means)AnyDescriptives + FrequenciesAnalyze > Descriptive Statistics > Frequencies / Descriptives

Common mistakes to avoid: running regression when you only hypothesised a correlation; running ANOVA on Likert scale means without checking group sizes (n < 20 per group is fragile); and running five different tests on the same data until something is significant — markers call this p-hacking. Pick one primary test per hypothesis and pre-register it in your methodology chapter.

If your supervisor asked for mediation, moderation, or factor analysis, that is a step beyond this guide — but the same workflow below still applies first. You cannot test a model on dirty, unreliable data.

2. The 5-Stage SPSS Survey Workflow (Beginner to Results)

Follow this order. Skipping straight to regression on raw Qualtrics data is the number one reason students get impossible outputs and supervisor rejections.

Stage 1 — Data cleanup and coding in SPSS

Before any test, your .sav file must be clean. See our full walkthrough: how to clean survey data in SPSS. The essentials:

  1. Variable View first: name variables (no spaces), set Type (Numeric), Label each question in full, set Values for every Likert point (1 = Strongly disagree … 5 = Strongly agree), and set Measure correctly — Nominal for gender/yes-no, Ordinal for education bands, Scale for Likert composites and age.
  2. Code missing data: use Transform > Recode into Same Variables to turn blanks, “Prefer not to say”, and straight-line speeders into system-missing. Never leave a 99 or 0 sitting in a Likert column — SPSS will treat it as a real answer and destroy your means.
  3. Reverse-score negative items: for items like “I feel disengaged at work” inside an engagement scale, go to Transform > Recode into Different Variables (5→1, 4→2, 2→4, 1→5). Forget this and your Cronbach’s alpha will collapse.
  4. Screen cases: run Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Frequencies on every variable and look for out-of-range values (a 6 on a 1–5 scale), Data > Identify Duplicate Cases, and flag straight-liners with identical answers across 20+ items.
  5. Create composites: once reliability passes, compute scale means via Transform > Compute Variable — e.g. Engagement = MEAN(Q1,Q2,Q3,Q4,Q5). Use MEAN not SUM so occasional missing items do not zero out the score.

Stage 2 — Descriptives: describe your sample properly

Path: Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Frequencies for categorical variables (tick Frequencies, Charts for bar charts) and Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Descriptives for continuous variables (tick Mean, Std. deviation, Minimum, Maximum, Skewness, Kurtosis).

Every dissertation needs a sample table: n, gender split, age M (SD), plus scale means. Skewness between −2 and +2 and kurtosis between −7 and +7 is a pragmatic normality screen for samples over 50. Report descriptives as M (SD) — for example, “Job satisfaction was moderate (M = 3.62, SD = 0.81).” Full templates are here: how to report SPSS results in APA 7.

Stage 3 — Reliability: Cronbach’s alpha before anything inferential

Path: Analyze > Scale > Reliability Analysis → move all items of one scale across → Model: Alpha → Statistics → tick Item, Scale, Scale if item deleted.

Read the Reliability Statistics box first: α ≥ .70 is acceptable for most dissertations, ≥ .80 is good, ≥ .90 may mean redundant items. Then check Item-Total Statistics: any item whose “Alpha if Item Deleted” jumps well above the overall alpha, or whose Corrected Item-Total Correlation is below .30, is a candidate for removal — but only if dropping it makes conceptual sense. Never delete items silently; report both alphas in your thesis. Detailed cut-offs and examples: Cronbach’s alpha in SPSS explained.

Stage 4 — Inferential tests: correlation, regression, t-test, ANOVA, chi-square

Correlation — Analyze > Correlate > Bivariate, tick Pearson + Spearman + two-tailed + Flag significant. Pearson needs two continuous variables with a roughly linear scatterplot; if Shapiro-Wilk is significant or data are ordinal, report Spearman’s rho instead. Effect sizes: r ≈ .10 small, ≈ .30 medium, ≈ .50 large.

Multiple regression — Analyze > Regression > Linear: DV into Dependent, predictors into Independent(s), Method = Enter. Click Statistics and tick Estimates, Confidence intervals, Model fit, R squared change, Descriptives, Collinearity diagnostics, Durbin-Watson. Click Plots and move *ZRESID to Y and *ZPRED to X, tick Histogram + Normal probability plot. This one dialog gives you R square, VIF, and Durbin-Watson in a single run. Full reading guide: how to interpret regression in SPSS.

T-test — Analyze > Compare Means > Independent-Samples T Test. Read Levene’s test first: if p > .05 use the top “equal variances assumed” row; if p ≤ .05 use the bottom row. Always report means, t, df, p, and Cohen’s d.

One-way ANOVA — Analyze > Compare Means > One-Way ANOVA → Options: Descriptives + Homogeneity of variance → Post Hoc: Tukey (equal variances) or Games-Howell (unequal). A significant omnibus F only tells you groups differ somewhere — the post-hoc table tells you where.

Chi-square — Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Crosstabs → Statistics: Chi-square + Phi and Cramer’s V → Cells: Observed + Expected + Row percentages. Check the footnote: no more than 20% of cells should have expected counts below 5, otherwise collapse categories or use Fisher’s exact test.

Stage 5 — Assumptions and APA reporting

No assumption checks, no defensible results chapter. The three markers ask about most:

  • Normality: Shapiro-Wilk p > .05, or Q-Q points hugging the line, or skewness/kurtosis in range for n > 50. Path: Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Explore > Plots → Normality plots with tests.
  • Multicollinearity (regression): VIF < 5 (strict supervisors want < 3), Tolerance > .20, and no predictor inter-correlation above .80. Fix by dropping or combining overlapping predictors.
  • Independence + homoscedasticity: Durbin-Watson near 2 (1.5–2.5 acceptable; near 0 or 4 signals autocorrelation) and a formless ZRESID-vs-ZPRED cloud. A funnel shape means heteroscedasticity — consider robust standard errors or transforming the DV.

Then convert every SPSS grid into a clean APA table — three horizontal lines only, no verticals, two decimals, italic M, SD, p, t, F, r, R², exact p-values (p = .032; never p = .000, write p < .001). Copy-paste guidance: report SPSS results in APA 7.

3. How to Read Key SPSS Outputs (Without Panicking)

Students paste output they do not understand. Learn to read four numbers and you can interpret 80% of survey dissertations:

OutputWhere to lookWhat counts as goodOne-line interpretation
Cronbach’s αReliability Statistics box≥ .70 acceptable, ≥ .80 good“The 5-item engagement scale showed acceptable reliability (α = .83).”
Pearson r + pCorrelations table, Sig. row|r| ≥ .30 meaningful; p < .05 significant“Engagement correlated positively with performance, r(148) = .42, p < .001.”
R² / Adjusted R²Model Summary box.02 small, .13 moderate, .26+ large“The model explained 34% of variance in turnover intention (R² = .34, adjusted R² = .32).”
VIF + Durbin-WatsonCoefficients + Model SummaryVIF < 5; DW ≈ 2“No multicollinearity (VIFs 1.1–1.8) and no autocorrelation (DW = 1.96).”
Beta (β) + pCoefficients table|β| larger = stronger; p < .05 = significant predictor“Workload was the strongest predictor (β = .38, p < .001); pay was not (β = .06, p = .412).”
t / F + pt-test / ANOVA tablesp < .05 = groups differ“Males scored higher than females, t(148) = 2.45, p = .015, d = 0.40.”

On p-values: p is the probability of seeing your result if the null were true — not the probability your hypothesis is true. Report exact values (p = .032), drop the leading zero per APA, and never write p = .000 (SPSS rounds; write p < .001). A p of .049 and .051 are virtually identical evidence — do not treat one as triumph and the other as disaster. Always pair p with an effect size (r, R², d, η²) so the marker sees practical importance, not just significance.

On R square: R² is variance explained, not causation. An R² of .34 means your predictors account for about a third of differences in the outcome — two-thirds remains unexplained, which is normal in social science. Report adjusted R² alongside it (it penalises extra predictors), and report which predictors were significant via their β weights, not just the overall F.

4. APA Reporting Basics: Sentences + Tables Markers Expect

Copy these patterns and fill in your numbers:

  • Correlation: “Pearson’s correlation showed a moderate positive association between remote-work frequency and job satisfaction, r(148) = .38, p < .001.”
  • Regression: “Multiple regression predicted turnover intention from workload, autonomy, and pay, F(3, 146) = 25.10, p < .001, R² = .34, adjusted R² = .32. Workload (β = .38, p < .001) and autonomy (β = −.29, p < .001) were significant; pay was not (β = .06, p = .412).”
  • T-test: “An independent-samples t-test showed males (M = 3.85, SD = 0.72) scored higher than females (M = 3.55, SD = 0.78), t(148) = 2.45, p = .015, d = 0.40.”
  • ANOVA: “One-way ANOVA showed differences in engagement across tenure groups, F(2, 147) = 6.12, p = .003, η² = .08. Tukey post-hoc tests showed …”
  • Chi-square: “Chi-square showed an association between contract type and turnover intention, χ²(1, N = 150) = 7.84, p = .005, V = .23.”

Formatting rules that cost easy marks: same font as body text, two decimals everywhere (except exact p < .001), italic statistical symbols, spaces around = and <, table number bold on its own line, italic table title below it, and an italic Note. line defining abbreviations.
