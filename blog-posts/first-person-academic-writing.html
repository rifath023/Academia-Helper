    Can You Use “I” in Academic Writing? When First Person Is Acceptable

    Understand when “I” and “we” are acceptable in university assignments, dissertations and reflective writing—and when your discipline may prefer a more impersonal academic voice.

    first person academic writingacademic voiceessay writingdissertation writinginternational students

    Quick Answer

    Can you use “I” in academic writing?

    Yes, first person can be acceptable in academic writing, but it depends on the assignment, discipline and university guidance. “I” is commonly used in reflective writing and may be appropriate in methodology, positionality or when explicitly stating an argument. In other contexts, a direct evidence-based sentence without “I” may be clearer.

    Why the Rules About First Person Differ

    Students are often given conflicting advice. One lecturer says never to write “I,” while another encourages a clear authorial voice. Both can be reasonable because academic writing conventions are not identical across disciplines or assignment types.

    A laboratory report may emphasise procedures and results, while a reflective assignment may require you to discuss your own experience. A qualitative dissertation may need a reflexivity section explaining how the researcher’s position influenced data collection. The correct choice therefore comes from context, not a universal ban on one pronoun.

    When First Person Is Often Acceptable

    Reflective Writing

    Reflection is about your experience, decisions, learning and development, so first person is usually natural: “I initially assumed…”, “I learned…”, or “I would approach the situation differently by…”. Removing “I” from reflective writing can make the account awkward or unclear.

    Research Methods and Methodology

    First person can be useful when you need to identify who made a research decision: “I conducted semi-structured interviews” or “I selected purposive sampling because…”. Some disciplines still prefer an impersonal form such as “Semi-structured interviews were conducted,” but the passive version can hide the researcher’s role.

    Positionality and Reflexivity

    In qualitative research, you may need to explain your relationship to participants, assumptions or influence on interpretation. First person is often the clearest way to do this because the researcher is part of the research process.

    Stating Your Argument

    In some humanities and social science writing, “I argue that…” is acceptable. However, it should introduce an argument you then support with evidence. “I feel that…” or “I personally think…” usually sounds less academic because it presents preference rather than reasoned analysis.

    When an Impersonal Style May Be Better

    If the sentence is about evidence rather than your personal action, you often do not need “I.” Compare “I think the results show a decline” with “The results show a decline.” The second sentence is shorter and puts the evidence first.

    Some scientific, technical, legal or business assignments may also have explicit style rules. A module guide might require formal third-person writing, or a journal-style report might expect conventions used in that field. Follow the brief even if you have seen different practices elsewhere.

    ⚠️

    Do not assume: “First person is always informal” and “First person is always acceptable” are both oversimplified rules. Your assignment instructions and discipline conventions should decide.

    Better Alternatives to “I Think” and “I Believe”

    Less effective wordingStronger academic option
    I think the policy is ineffective.The policy appears ineffective because…
    I believe this study is limited.A key limitation of the study is…
    In my opinion, the results are important.The results are significant because…
    I can see that the authors disagree.The authors differ in their interpretation of…

    These revisions do not remove your judgement. They make the reasoning visible. Our guide to academic sentence starters provides additional language for analysis, comparison and evaluation.

    Can You Use First Person in a Dissertation?

    Many dissertations use first person selectively. A literature review may focus mainly on published research, while the methodology may use “I” to explain design choices. A qualitative study may use it more frequently in reflexive discussion, whereas some quantitative disciplines prefer impersonal wording.

    The most important point is consistency with your department’s expectations. Check dissertation handbooks, recent examples from your programme, supervisor guidance and the required style. Do not copy the pronoun style of an unrelated subject just because it looks academic.

    What About “We”?

    If a paper genuinely has several authors, “we” can accurately describe shared actions: “We collected the data” or “We propose…”. If you are the only author, using “we” simply to avoid “I” can create confusion. Some disciplines use “we” rhetorically, as in “We can now consider…”, but this is a stylistic convention rather than a requirement.

    For an individual university assignment, clarity is usually more important than trying to imitate the voice of a multi-author journal article.

    How First Person Fits Academic Voice

    Academic voice comes from evidence, precision, organisation and reasoning—not from avoiding a particular pronoun. You can write a weak third-person paragraph or a strong first-person paragraph. What matters is whether the reader can see the claim, evidence and logic.

    First person also does not remove the need for cautious claims. If your evidence is limited, “I argue” should not become a licence to overstate. Review our guide to hedging in academic writing for ways to express appropriate levels of certainty.

    Practical Checklist Before You Use “I”

    • Check the assignment brief and module handbook.
    • Identify the assignment type: reflective, analytical, research report or dissertation.
    • Check conventions in your discipline.
    • Use first person when your role or decision genuinely matters.
    • Remove “I think” when the evidence itself can lead the sentence.
    • Keep your pronoun choice consistent unless different sections require different styles.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Can I use “I” in a university essay?
    Sometimes. First person may be acceptable when stating your argument, explaining a choice or writing in a discipline that allows authorial voice. Always check the assignment brief, marking criteria and your department’s guidance.
    Can I use first person in a dissertation?
    Yes in many dissertations, especially in methodology, reflexivity or positionality sections where you explain decisions you made. Conventions vary by discipline, so follow your supervisor and programme guidance.
    Is “I think” academic?
    Usually it is better to make the argument directly and support it with evidence. Instead of “I think this policy is ineffective,” write “This policy appears ineffective because…”. Use “I argue” only when first person is appropriate and the authorial role matters.
    Should I use “we” if I am the only author?
    Usually no. Do not use “we” to sound more academic if only one person completed the work. Some disciplines use an inclusive “we” to guide the reader, but this is style-dependent.
    Why do some lecturers tell students never to use “I”?
    Some subjects and assignment types prefer an impersonal style, while others accept or encourage first person. The rule is not universal, so local guidance from the module or department should take priority.
    What can I write instead of “I believe”?
    Often you can remove the phrase and state the evidence-based claim directly. For example, “The evidence suggests…” or “This analysis indicates…” is usually stronger than “I believe the evidence shows…”.
