What To Do When a Group Member Does Nothing: Email Templates to Your Lecturer (UK)

Group member not contributing and deadline is getting closer? Here is exactly what to do, what to say, and how to email your professor about a group member — with 3 copy-paste UK templates that protect your grade without sounding like you are snitching.

group member not contributing what to do email professor group member peer evaluation wording group project conflict UK university

You did the reading. You turned up to every Teams call. You have a shared Google Doc with your name all over the version history — and one group member doing nothing at all. No messages, no draft, no apology. Just silence while your deadline burns.

If you are searching for group member not contributing what to do, you are not alone. Every UK university from Manchester to King's College London deals with free-riding every semester. Lecturers know it happens. What separates students who keep their First from those who get dragged down is how they handle it: calmly, in writing, and early enough for the tutor to actually help.

This guide shows you exactly how to email your professor about a group member in the UK — without sounding petty, aggressive, or unprofessional. You will get a 4-step escalation plan used by successful groups, 3 copy-paste email templates, peer evaluation wording that works, and a checklist of evidence UK markers actually trust.

For wider context on surviving dysfunctional groups, see our guides on what to do when a group assignment member is not contributing and how peer evaluation works. If you are unsure about the brief itself, read how to email your lecturer for assignment clarification first.

Quick answer: Do not email your lecturer on day one. Talk to the member privately → document everything in writing → send one clear written warning with a 48-hour deadline → then email your tutor with module code, timeline, and screenshots. That evidence trail is what unlocks peer-moderated mark adjustments in most UK unis.

How UK Unis Actually Handle Group Project Conflict

Before you write anything, understand what your lecturer can do. UK group policies fall into three common models. Your module handbook will state which one applies — check it before you email:

  • 1. Shared group mark only: Everyone gets the same mark. Harsh, but still common in first-year modules. Tutors can rarely change marks here unless there is formal misconduct, but they can mediate, reallocate tasks, or allow a contribution appendix.
  • 2. Group mark + peer moderation (most common): You submit a peer evaluation via Turnitin, BuddyCheck, WebPA, or a department form. Tools like WebPA automatically scale individual marks up or down based on peer ratings. This is where peer evaluation wording directly affects grades.
  • 3. Split mark (product + process): For example, 70% group report + 30% individual reflection, logbook, or viva. Even if the free-rider coasts on the report, your individual component can still protect your grade — if you evidence your own contribution.

Most Russell Group and post-92 universities now use option 2 or 3 precisely because of group project conflict. At Birmingham, Leeds, UCL and similar institutions, tutors routinely tell students: we cannot adjust what we cannot see. That means chat logs, task boards, and version history are not optional extras — they are your grade insurance.

UK tutors do not want drama. They want dates, tasks, and proof you gave the person a fair chance to catch up. Provide that, and they will help you.

The 4-Step Escalation Plan (Talk → Document → Warn → Email Tutor)

Do not jump straight to emailing your lecturer. Follow this order. Each step creates the paper trail the next step needs.

1

Step 1: Talk Privately First (Not in the Group Chat)

A public call-out in the group WhatsApp makes people defensive. Message the person one-to-one, assume a benign reason first — illness, family emergency, confusion about the task — and offer a concrete lifeline.

Copy-paste script for WhatsApp / Teams DM:

“Hi [Name], hope you’re okay — we’ve missed you in the last two meetings. You were down for [Section 2 – literature review, ~800 words, due Friday]. Is everything alright? If you’re stuck I can share my notes to get you started. Could you let us know by tomorrow if you can still do it, or if we need to swap tasks? No judgement, we just need to plan. Thanks!”

Why this works: it is kind, it names the exact task and deadline, and it sets a reply deadline. Screenshot it regardless of whether they reply.

2

Step 2: Document Everything From Now On

From this point, nothing lives in verbal calls or disappearing voice notes. UK tutors trust timestamps. Create a simple evidence trail:

  • Task allocation table: Who → what → word count → due date. Put it in Teams, Notion, Trello, or even a pinned WhatsApp message. Example: Priya – Methodology – 1000 words – due 18 Nov.
  • Meeting log: Date, attendees, absentees, decisions. One line per meeting is enough.
  • Chat logs: Keep all nudges in the main group chat so the silence is visible. Export as PDF later (WhatsApp → Chat Info → Export Chat).
  • Work proof: Use Google Docs or OneDrive so Version History shows who wrote what and when. Trello / Jira board movement history also counts.

If you already have a group assignment member not contributing, start this log today — even two weeks of clean evidence is far stronger than “he never does anything” with no dates.

3

Step 3: Send One Formal Written Warning (48-Hour Rule)

If private contact fails, send one clear, polite warning in the group channel so all members witness it. Give a specific, achievable catch-up task and a 48-hour deadline. This is the message tutors love to see, because it proves fairness.

Script:

“Hi everyone, quick update for transparency. [Name], we haven’t received [Section 2 draft due 15 Nov] and you missed meetings on [10th + 13th Nov] after reminders on [11th + 14th]. To keep us on track for the [22 Nov] deadline, could you please submit [500-word bullet draft of Section 2] by [17 Nov, 6pm]? If we don’t hear from you by then, we’ll have to reallocate and flag reduced contribution to [Dr Smith] so marks stay fair. Happy to help if you’re stuck — just let us know today.”

Do not threaten, insult, or remove them from the doc. Reassign work quietly after the deadline passes and note it in your log.

4

Step 4: Email Your Tutor / Lecturer (With Evidence)

Only now should you email your professor about the group member. Email early enough to allow intervention — ideally 7–10 days before submission, not the night before. CC all contributing members, attach evidence, and ask for guidance rather than punishment. Use Template 2 below.

If your module uses peer evaluation, mention you will reflect this honestly in the peer review. If you need to clarify the brief while you cover extra work, use our email lecturer for assignment clarification template in the same week.

💬 Tutor Insight

Lecturers consistently say the same thing in staff forums: students who email with “X did Y on Z date, here are screenshots, we offered help twice, what should we do?” get fast, supportive replies. Students who email “X is lazy, remove him” get told to resolve it themselves. Tone + timeline = outcome.

3 Copy-Paste Email Templates (UK English)

Replace everything in [brackets]. Keep subject lines exactly as shown — lecturers filter by module code. All three are written in formal UK academic English (Dear Dr / Kind regards, not Hey Prof).

Template 1: Polite Nudge to the Group Member (Before You Involve the Tutor)

Use this as an email if they ignore chat. It creates a formal timestamp outside WhatsApp.

📧 Template 1 — Member Nudge Copy & Send

Subject: [BUS2014] Group 6 – Quick check-in on your section

Hi [Name],

I hope you’re well. I’m writing because we haven’t heard from you since [12 November] and we missed you at our meetings on [13 and 15 November].

As agreed on [8 November], you kindly took on [Section 2.2 – competitor analysis, approx. 700 words, due 16 November]. The rest of the group have submitted drafts, and we need your section to link the introduction and conclusion together.

If you’re having difficulties — workload, health, or unclear brief — please let me know today and I can share my sources or swap to a smaller task ([e.g. proofreading + references]). Could you please reply by [17 November, 5pm] to confirm whether you can submit a draft?

We want to keep this fair for everyone and avoid involving [Dr Smith] unnecessarily. Thanks for letting us know either way.

Best wishes,
[Your Full Name]
[Module Code + Group Number]
[Your University Email] | [Phone]

Tip: BCC yourself and keep the sent email. If they reply with abuse, do not engage — forward to Template 2.

Template 2: Formal Email to Tutor / Lecturer / Module Leader

This is the main email professor group member template. Send from your university email, CC contributing teammates, attach 2–4 pages of evidence max (not 60 screenshots).

📧 Template 2 — Formal Tutor Email Most Used

To: Dr [Smith] – [module.code@university.ac.uk]
CC: [contributing teammates]
Subject: [BUS2014] Group 6 – Request for guidance on unequal contribution (deadline 28 Nov)

Dear Dr [Smith],

I am writing on behalf of Group 6 for [BUS2014 Strategic Management, Seminar B] to seek your guidance regarding unequal contribution ahead of our submission on [28 November].

Summary: Despite repeated reminders, [Full Name of Member, Student ID if known] has not submitted any work to date. We have reallocated tasks to protect the submission, but we wish to ensure marks fairly reflect contribution.

Timeline of what we have done:

• [8 Nov] – Tasks allocated in Teams (screenshot App. A): [Name] – Section 2.2, 700 words, due 16 Nov.
• [13 + 15 Nov] – Did not attend group meetings (attendance log App. B).
• [14 Nov] – Private check-in via WhatsApp offering help – no reply (App. C).
• [16 Nov] – Group warning with 48-hour extension to 18 Nov, 6pm – no submission (App. D).
• [19 Nov] – Tasks reallocated: [Priya took 400 words, I took 300 words]. Version history attached (App. E).

We have tried to resolve this internally and remain willing to include [Name] if they contribute before [24 Nov]. Could you please advise on next steps — whether to note this in the peer evaluation / contribution statement, or whether you would prefer a brief meeting?

Attached: task allocation, meeting log, exported chat (highlighted), version history PDF.

Thank you for your time and understanding. We appreciate any guidance.

Kind regards,
[Your Full Name, Student ID]
On behalf of [Names of Contributing Members]
[Module, Group, University]

Keep to under 300 words. Never demand “remove them” or “give them zero” — ask for guidance. Tutors decide penalties.

Template 3: Confidential Peer Evaluation Wording (WebPA / BuddyCheck / Turnitin)

When you reach the peer evaluation wording stage, honesty matters — but so does professionalism. Vague revenge scores (“0 for everything, he’s useless”) get discounted by moderators. Specific behavioural wording gets upheld.

📧 Template 3 — Peer-Eval Comments Confidential

Scores example (0–5 scale): Contribution 1/5, Communication 1/5, Reliability 1/5, Quality N/A (no work submitted), Teamwork 2/5.

Confidential comment to tutor (copy-paste and adapt):

“[Name] was allocated Section 2.2 (700 words, due 16 Nov, extended to 18 Nov). Despite a private check-in on 14 Nov and a group warning on 16 Nov, no draft was submitted and two meetings (13 + 15 Nov) were missed without apology. The section was reallocated to two members to meet the 28 Nov deadline. Google Docs version history shows no edits from [Name]. I have attached chat exports to my tutor email of 19 Nov. I rate contribution accordingly and remain willing to collaborate if circumstances change.”

For contributing members, write the opposite:

“[Priya] completed her own section plus 400 words of reallocated work, attended all meetings, and proofread the final draft. 5/5 across all criteria.”

Rate yourself honestly too. Giving yourself 5/5 while giving everyone else 1/5 looks biased. Moderators compare patterns across the whole group.

What NOT to Do (Even If You Are Furious)

A group project conflict can feel deeply unfair, especially when your degree classification is on the line. But these mistakes actively harm your case with UK lecturers:

  • Do not dox or harass them. No named shaming on course group chats, TikTok, or department forums. It can breach your university’s bullying and harassment policy and flip you from victim to perpetrator.
  • Do not lock them out of documents. Removing them from Google Drive or Teams looks like exclusion. Keep access open and let version history prove non-participation.
  • Do not submit without their name to “punish” them. Unless your tutor explicitly authorises it, removing a registered group member can be treated as academic misconduct. Always ask first.
  • Do not lie in peer review. Do not exaggerate (“never replied once” when they replied twice) or get friends to mass-downvote. WebPA flags suspicious patterns and tutors will check logs.
  • Do not email the Vice-Chancellor on day one. Follow the chain: seminar tutor → module leader → personal tutor. Skipping levels annoys decision-makers.
  • Do not do all their work silently. If you cover everything without flagging, the tutor assumes the group functioned. Flag early, reallocate transparently, and record who did what.

Final Thoughts: Protect Your Grade Without Burning Bridges

A group member not contributing feels personal, but treat it as a process problem. Talk privately, document relentlessly, warn once in writing, then email your professor about the group member with facts — not frustration. That single, well-evidenced email does more for your mark than ten angry WhatsApps.

Use the templates above today: send the nudge now, set your 48-hour deadline, and prepare Template 2 so you are ready a week before submission. Pair honest peer evaluation wording with clean version history, and most UK tutors will ensure fairness — whether through WebPA moderation, contribution statements, or individual mark adjustments.

You should not have to carry an entire group project alone while someone else coasts. If you have reallocated work, lost sleep, and still face a tight deadline, get support early rather than submitting exhausted, under-referenced work that undersells your effort.
