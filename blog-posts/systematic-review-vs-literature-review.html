    Systematic Review vs Literature Review: What's the Difference?

    Understand the key differences between a systematic review and a literature review. Learn which one suits your dissertation, the types of literature review, and when to use each.

    systematic reviewliterature reviewscoping reviewPRISMAresearch methodologydissertation

    Two of the most commonly confused terms in academic research are "systematic review" and "literature review." Both involve reviewing existing research — but they are fundamentally different in their purpose, rigour, and methodology. Choosing the wrong one for your dissertation could result in a misaligned methodology chapter and significant wasted effort.

    This guide explains the key differences clearly, helps you understand which is appropriate for your level of study, and provides practical guidance for each. For expert dissertation support, Academia Helper is here to help.

    Quick Summary: A literature review is a selective, thematic synthesis of existing research used to contextualise a study. A systematic review follows a rigorous, pre-registered protocol to exhaustively identify and synthesise all evidence on a specific question. They serve different purposes and require different levels of rigour.

    At a Glance: The Key Differences

    FeatureLiterature ReviewSystematic Review
    PurposeContextualise a study; identify gapsAnswer a specific research question exhaustively
    ProtocolFlexible, informalPre-registered, explicit, reproducible
    Search strategySelective, not exhaustiveComprehensive, documented in full
    Inclusion criteriaInformal, researcher's judgementExplicit, pre-defined criteria
    ScreeningSingle reviewerTwo independent reviewers
    Quality appraisalOptional, informalMandatory, using validated tools
    Bias riskHigher (selection bias possible)Lower (bias minimised by protocol)

    Time requiredWeeks to a few monthsMonths to years

    Evidence levelLower (narrative synthesis)Highest (top of evidence hierarchy)

    What a Literature Review Is For

    A literature review — specifically a narrative or critical literature review — is typically Chapter 2 of a dissertation or thesis. Its purpose is to:

    • Map the existing body of knowledge on your topic
    • Identify key theories, frameworks, and debates
    • Highlight the research gap your study will address
    • Justify your research question and methodology choices

    It is selective by design. You choose the most relevant and high-quality sources, organise them thematically, and build a critical argument that leads to your research question. It does not attempt to identify every study ever published on the topic.

    What a Systematic Review Is For

    A systematic review is itself the entire research output — not just a chapter within a larger study. It answers a very specific, pre-defined research question by exhaustively identifying and synthesising all available evidence. The goal is to provide the most complete, unbiased answer possible to that question.

    ℹ️

    Key distinction: A literature review leads into your research. A systematic review is your research. In a dissertation using a systematic review methodology, the systematic review is the empirical contribution — there is no separate data collection chapter.

    Which Should Students Use?

    🎓

    Undergraduate Dissertations

    Use a narrative/critical literature review as Chapter 2. Conduct primary research (interviews, survey) as your empirical contribution. A systematic review methodology is rare and ambitious at this level.

    📚

    Master's Dissertations

    Use a narrative literature review in Chapter 2 alongside primary research — or use a simplified systematic review as your entire methodology if your research question suits it. The latter is increasingly accepted and valued.

    🔬

    PhD Theses

    A chapter-level narrative literature review is standard. A systematic review chapter or standalone systematic review paper is common and appropriate, particularly in health, education, and social sciences.

    Types of Literature Review

    Narrative

    Narrative Review

    Thematic, selective synthesis. Most common in dissertations. Builds a critical argument. No formal protocol required.

    Systematic

    Systematic Review

    Exhaustive, protocol-driven. Answers a specific question. Requires PRISMA reporting and dual screening.

    Scoping

    Scoping Review

    Maps the breadth of literature on a broad topic. Less rigorous than systematic but more structured than narrative. Useful when the field is emerging.

    Meta-Analysis

    Meta-Analysis

    Statistical pooling of results from multiple studies. Requires sufficient similarity between studies. Often published alongside a systematic review.

    Integrative

    Integrative Review

    Combines diverse study designs (quantitative and qualitative). Useful for complex topics requiring multiple types of evidence.

    Rapid

    Rapid Review

    Streamlined systematic review for time-limited contexts. Used in policy and practice settings where speed is essential. Less exhaustive than full systematic review.

    Final Thoughts

    The core difference is simple: a literature review builds context for your research; a systematic review is your research. Understanding this distinction helps you design a dissertation methodology that is coherent, appropriately rigorous, and matched to your research question.     Frequently Asked Questions

    Can a dissertation use a systematic review instead of primary research?
    Yes. A systematic review is a recognised and highly regarded dissertation methodology, particularly at Master's and PhD level. It is the entire empirical contribution — there is no separate data collection chapter. You answer your research question by synthesising existing evidence.
    Is a systematic review harder than a literature review?
    Yes — significantly. A systematic review requires a pre-registered protocol, exhaustive searching of multiple databases, dual screening, formal quality appraisal, and PRISMA reporting. A narrative literature review is more flexible and less time-intensive.
    What is a scoping review?
    A scoping review maps the breadth and nature of evidence on a broad topic without the full rigour of a systematic review. It is useful when exploring an emerging field or when the purpose is to identify gaps and types of evidence available, rather than to answer a specific clinical or policy question.
    Do I need PRISMA for a student systematic review?
    Yes. Even for student dissertations, using the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram and checklist is strongly recommended. It demonstrates methodological rigour and is the expected reporting standard for any systematic review, regardless of whether it is published or conducted for a dissertation.
