How to Apply Theory to a Case Study Example (HR, Nursing, Business – 2026 Guide)

Struggling with “apply theory to the case”? Learn how to apply theory to a case study with a 4-step framework, full HR, business and nursing paragraph examples, sentence starters, and the mistakes that cost marks.

If your feedback says “descriptive – no application of theory”, you are not alone. Most UK business, HR and nursing undergraduates know what Maslow, Herzberg, Porter or Orem said, but lose marks because they never show how that theory explains this specific case. Learning how to apply theory to a case study is the single fastest way to move from a 2:2 to a 2:1.

This 2026 guide gives you a repeatable 4-step framework, three full worked theory application paragraph examples (HR, business and nursing), a bank of sentence starters for case study analysis, and a checklist you can use on every assignment. What Does “Apply Theory” Actually Mean?

Describing is summarising the textbook: “Herzberg (1966) identified hygiene factors and motivators.” Applying is using that idea as a lens to interpret case evidence: “The 5% pay rise at NorthCare failed because pay is a hygiene factor; it removed dissatisfaction but, without recognition or advancement, could not create motivation – which explains why turnover stayed at 32%.”

Think of it as a formula UK markers love: Theory concept + Case fact + Explanation of link + Judgement. Every strong theory application paragraph example in this guide follows that order. The theory explains, the case proves, and you interpret. If a sentence could appear in any essay about Herzberg without the case, it is description. If it only makes sense with your case facts, it is application.

A useful ratio is 20% description, 80% application. Spend one or two sentences defining the concept accurately with a citation (Herzberg, 1966; Mullins, 2019), then devote the rest of the paragraph to mapping, analysing and evaluating. Examiners at Level 5 and 6 explicitly reward “critical application” – showing where the theory fits well and where it breaks down in context. A paragraph that only lists theory, even accurately, caps you at a pass.

The 4-Step Framework to Apply Any Theory

Use these four steps for every theory paragraph, whether you need to apply HR theory to a case example, a Porter analysis, or a nursing model. It works for 200-word paragraphs and 1,000-word sections – just expand each step.

Step 1 – Describe the theory briefly (and correctly)

Define only the part you will use, with a citation. If you are using Herzberg, do not rehearse his whole biography – state that hygiene factors (pay, policy, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate, while motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility) drive satisfaction (Herzberg, 1966). If you are using Porter’s Five Forces (1979), define the one force you are analysing, for example buyer power. Accuracy matters: confusing hygiene factors with motivators, or calling a SWOT factor a “force”, signals to the marker that the rest of the application cannot be trusted. One to two sentences is enough.

Step 2 – Select your lens and justify it

Tell the reader why this theory is the right tool for this question. For example: “Herzberg is appropriate here because the case centres on a pay-led retention strategy that has failed, which is precisely the hygiene–motivator distinction the theory addresses.” This justification sentence separates first-class work from average work. It shows academic judgement rather than random theory-dropping. If the brief asks you to compare lenses, justify each: Maslow for basic-needs context, Herzberg for job-design detail. For more on choosing lenses, see how to choose the right theory for a case study.

Step 3 – Map concepts to case facts and analyse

This is where marks are won. Take each element of the theory and attach it to a specific, cited fact. Do not write “staff are unhappy”. Write “healthcare assistants cite rota changes without consultation (Case Appendix B, p.2) and a 32% twelve-month turnover rate”. Then explain the mechanism: why does the theory predict this outcome? “Because unpredictable rotas undermine the hygiene factor of working conditions, Herzberg predicts dissatisfaction will persist even after the 5% uplift – which is exactly what the exit-interview data shows.” Use connectives that force analysis: “this suggests”, “this explains”, “consequently”, “which aligns with”. Each mapping should be a mini-argument, not a label.

Step 4 – Evaluate fit and recommend

Finish by judging the theory: what does it illuminate well, and what does it miss? No theory fits perfectly. Herzberg was developed with US engineers and accountants in the 1960s and underplays culture, gig-economy precarity and intrinsic public-service motivation in NHS settings (critique via Mullins, 2019). Porter’s Five Forces is a snapshot and underplays digital disruption unless combined with PESTLE. Stating one strength and one limitation, then linking to a recommendation (“therefore NorthCare should redesign roles around recognition and autonomy, not a further blanket pay rise”), demonstrates the critical evaluation UK rubrics allocate 20–30% of marks to. Structure each paragraph using PEEL or TEEL paragraph structure so evaluation lands in the final “Link” sentence.

Worked Example 1: HR – Herzberg at NorthCare (Apply HR Theory Case Example)

Case snapshot — NorthCare NHS Trust (fictionalised brief)

NorthCare gave all Band 2–3 staff a 5% pay uplift in 2024 to cut turnover. Twelve months later turnover remains 32%, exit interviews cite “no recognition, no progression, rotas changed at short notice”. Sickness absence is 7.1% vs 5.4% NHS average. Your task: apply motivation theory to explain why the pay strategy failed and recommend action.

Why Herzberg (not just Maslow)? Maslow’s hierarchy (1943) is useful for broad needs, but Herzberg’s two-factor theory (1966) directly distinguishes pay (hygiene) from recognition and advancement (motivators) – exactly the puzzle in the case. Mention Maslow once for context if you wish, but anchor the paragraph in Herzberg.

✕ Before — descriptive (50–55%)

“Herzberg had hygiene factors and motivators. Hygiene factors include pay and working conditions. Motivators include recognition. NorthCare has high turnover. Motivation is important for retention.”

✓ After — applied (65%+)

“Herzberg’s two-factor theory (1966) helps explain why NorthCare’s 5% pay uplift failed to cut 32% turnover. Herzberg argues pay and working conditions are hygiene factors that prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate, while recognition and advancement are motivators that drive effort (Mullins, 2019). At NorthCare, the uplift addressed the hygiene factor of salary, yet exit interviews cite ‘no recognition’ and ‘no progression’, and rotas changed at short notice (Appendix B) – a second hygiene failure. This suggests dissatisfaction persisted on two fronts while motivators remained absent, so Herzberg predicts exactly the observed outcome: turnover unchanged and absence at 7.1%. The theory fits well for diagnosing the pay fallacy, though its 1960s US engineer sample limits direct transfer to NHS support workers, where public-service motivation also matters. NorthCare should therefore freeze further blanket rises and invest in structured recognition, band-3 development pathways and participative rostering.”

Why the “after” scores: it defines the theory in one sentence, maps both concepts to cited facts (pay uplift → hygiene; no recognition/progression → motivators; rota chaos → working conditions), explains the causal mechanism (“so Herzberg predicts exactly…”), evaluates a limitation, and ends with a theory-driven recommendation. To strengthen it further, integrate a second piece of evidence – e.g., NHS Staff Survey recognition scores – using the same map–analyse pattern.

Worked Example 2: Business – Porter’s Five Forces on a DTC Coffee Brand

Case snapshot — BrewBox (fictionalised brief)

BrewBox is a UK direct-to-consumer compostable-pod coffee brand. Revenue £4.2m, growth stalled at 3%. Two supermarkets demand 40% margin and threaten delisting; Nespresso-compatible pods face 12 rival entrants; switching costs are near zero; aluminium-pod suppliers raised prices 18%. Task: use Porter to assess industry attractiveness and advise on strategy.

Students often list all five forces in bullet points with no verdict. Instead, apply two forces in depth per paragraph and always end with “so what for BrewBox?”.

✕ Before — list, no application

“Porter has five forces: buyer power, supplier power, new entrants, substitutes and rivalry. BrewBox has buyers and suppliers. Competition is high. The industry is competitive.”

✓ After — applied

“Porter’s Five Forces (1979) suggests BrewBox operates in an unattractive segment dominated by high buyer power and low entry barriers. Buyer power is strong because two grocery multiples account for 55% of wholesale volume and demand 40% margin while credibly threatening delisting (Case Exhibit 2); with near-zero switching costs for consumers, BrewBox cannot pass on the 18% supplier price rise without volume loss. The threat of new entrants is likewise high: compostable-pod tooling is widely available, evidenced by 12 entrants in 18 months, so Porter predicts margin compression unless BrewBox differentiates. Together these forces explain stalled 3% growth despite a growing category. Porter’s snapshot fits the current negotiation well but captures digital subscription dynamics weakly, so BrewBox should reduce buyer dependence by scaling DTC subscriptions and build a resource-based moat – proprietary compostable-barrier IP and a recycling partnership – rather than competing on supermarket price.”

Markers’ notes: the strong version quantifies power (55%, 40%, 18%, 12 entrants), uses Porter’s causal language (“predicts margin compression unless…”), and converts analysis into strategy. Add a mini-SWOT only after Porter has done the external work – SWOT lists internal strengths without Porter’s industry logic and on its own rarely earns analysis marks. Cite Porter (1979) or Johnson et al. (2020) for the framework and the case exhibits for every number.

Worked Example 3: Nursing – Roper, Logan & Tierney + Orem on Post-Op Care

Case snapshot — Mrs A, 68, Day 2 post hip arthroplasty

Mrs A (68) is Day 2 post elective hip arthroplasty. Observations stable, pain 6/10 on movement, BMI 31, lives alone, daughter visits weekends. She refuses to mobilise to the chair, fears falling, missed physiotherapy session. Wound dry, TED stockings on, fluids adequate. Task: apply a nursing model to assess needs and plan person-centred care.

For UK nursing case studies, Roper, Logan and Tierney’s Activities of Living (2000) is ideal for holistic assessment, while Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory (1991) justifies the level of nursing intervention. Use Roper to assess, Orem to decide how much to intervene.

✕ Before — generic, unsafe

“Orem is about self-care. Roper has 12 activities of living. Mrs A needs care after her operation. Nurses should help her mobilise and give pain relief.”

✓ After — applied, person-centred

“The Roper–Logan–Tierney model (Roper et al., 2000) frames Mrs A’s reluctance to mobilise as an interdependence between the activities of mobilising, maintaining a safe environment and communicating. On assessment, mobilising is dependent (refuses chair transfer, missed physiotherapy, pain 6/10 on movement), which compromises safety (falls risk, VTE risk despite TED stockings) and is influenced by psychological factors (fear of falling) and social context (lives alone, limited weekday support). Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory (1991) clarifies the nursing response: Mrs A has a temporary wholly compensatory need for VTE prophylaxis monitoring and a partly compensatory need for mobilisation, requiring a supportive-educative approach rather than doing everything for her. The nurse should therefore negotiate a graded mobilisation goal using the NMC Code’s person-centred partnership (NMC, 2018) – optimise multimodal analgesia 30 minutes before physiotherapy, demonstrate chair-transfer technique, and co-produce a weekend daughter-supported walking plan – then evaluate against reduced pain scores and distance mobilised. Roper captures breadth well but risks checklist use; Orem corrects this by calibrating independence, which protects Mrs A’s dignity while meeting post-arthroplasty VTE guidance (NICE NG89).”

Why this passes OSCE-style scrutiny: it names specific activities, links biological, psychological, sociocultural and environmental factors (the five Roper factors), applies Orem’s three systems accurately, cites NMC and NICE, and ends with measurable evaluation. Never invent observations – only use data the case gives, and flag what you would still assess (e.g., MUST score, continence, cognition) to show safe practice.

Sentence Starters Bank for Case Study Analysis

Copy these sentence starters for case study analysis into your paragraphs. Each one forces you to connect theory and evidence rather than describe. Combine one starter from each row to build a complete theory application paragraph example.

MoveSentence starters (pick one per paragraph)
Introduce lens“[Theory] is a useful lens here because…”; “Viewed through [concept], the case suggests…”; “Of the competing explanations, [theory] best fits because…”
Define briefly“[Author] (year) defines [concept] as…”; “Central to [theory] is the distinction between X and Y…”; “The key proposition used here is that…”
Map to fact“This is evident in… (Appendix X)”; “The case fact that [statistic/quote] corresponds to [concept]…”; “For example, [fact] illustrates…”
Analyse mechanism“This explains why…”; “This suggests that…”; “Consequently, [theory] predicts…”; “Which aligns with / contradicts…”
Evaluate fit“The theory illuminates X well, but overlooks…”; “A limitation in this context is…”; “An alternative reading via [theory B] would argue…”
Recommend / conclude“Therefore, the theory-informed priority is…”; “It follows that [organisation] should…”; “On this analysis, the most defensible option is…”

Example chain in action: “Herzberg is a useful lens here because the case centres on failed pay-led retention. Herzberg (1966) distinguishes hygiene factors from motivators. The 32% turnover alongside ‘no recognition’ (Appendix B) corresponds to absent motivators. This explains why the uplift did not motivate. The theory fits the pay puzzle but overlooks public-service motivation. Therefore NorthCare should invest in recognition pathways.” Five sentences, full application. For paragraph flow, pair these with PEEL topic-sentence and linking techniques.

7 Mistakes That Signal “Name-Dropping Theory”

MistakeWhy it loses marksFix
1. Textbook dump300 words on Maslow, two lines on the case.Cap definition at 2 sentences; spend 80% on mapping.
2. Theory shoppingFive theories named, none applied.One theory per paragraph; justify selection.
3. Vague facts“Staff are unhappy” – unverifiable.Cite named facts: 32%, 7.1%, Appendix B quote.
4. No mechanismLabels without “why” (“this is a hygiene factor” – full stop).Add “which predicts / explains… because…”.
5. No evaluationTreats theory as universal truth.One strength + one limit in context.
6. Wrong levelPorter for individual motivation; Maslow for industry structure.Match lens to question level (individual / firm / industry).
7. No recommendationAnalysis floats free of action.End every section: “therefore X should…”.

The most common is No. 4. Markers circle “so what?” next to paragraphs that correctly label a hygiene factor but never explain how it produces the observed turnover. Always complete the causal sentence. If you cannot state the mechanism in one line, you have not yet applied the theory.

Checklist: Is Your Paragraph Really Applied?

  • ☐ Does the topic sentence name both the theory and the case issue it explains?
  • ☐ Is the definition ≤2 sentences, accurate, and cited (theorist + textbook)?
  • ☐ Have you mapped every key concept to a named, cited case fact (not “staff / patients generally”)?
  • ☐ Have you explained the mechanism with “this suggests / predicts / explains why”?
  • ☐ Have you evaluated one strength and one limitation in this context?
  • ☐ Does the paragraph end with a theory-driven recommendation or judgement?
  • ☐ Could this paragraph only have been written about your case? If yes – it is applied.

Run this checklist on every theory paragraph before submission. If you tick all seven, you have met the “critical application” criterion that separates merit from distinction at most UK universities. Keep your evidence tightly linked using evidence-integration techniques so quotations and statistics do analytical work rather than padding word count.

Final Thoughts

Knowing how to apply theory to a case study is a skill, not a talent. Describe briefly, justify your lens, map every concept to a cited fact, explain the mechanism, and evaluate fit. Whether your apply HR theory case example uses Herzberg, your strategy section uses Porter, or your nursing essay uses Roper and Orem, the same four steps earn the analysis marks.

Start your next case study by writing one “after” paragraph using the sentence starters above – then use the checklist to confirm it could only have been written about your case. Do that consistently, and “limited application of theory” will disappear from your feedback for good.
