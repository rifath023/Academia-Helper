Maintenance Loan Not Enough UK: Work & Saving Tips 2026

If your maintenance loan is not enough, you are not alone. We unpack the 2026 shortfall, safe part-time hours and 12 saving tips that actually close the gap.

maintenance loan not enoughstudent part time work ukstudent saving tips ukstudent budget

Is your maintenance loan not enough in 2026? The numbers say it probably is not. Save the Student's 2025 survey of 1,151 students (June–August 2025) found the loan falls £502 short every month — which is why 58% of students now work part-time, 30% run a side hustle, 51% dip into savings and 34% use an overdraft. Loughborough University and HEPI analysis puts the deeper benchmark even higher: the minimum income standard is £366 per week (£18,632 a year outside London, £21,774 in London), maximum support covers only about 55%, and the annual shortfall reaches £8,405. This guide explains how the loan is calculated, shows the shortfall maths, sets safe student part-time work limits, and gives 12 student saving tips plus the bursaries and help funds most students miss.

How the maintenance loan is calculated

Your maintenance loan is means-tested, so two students on the same course can receive very different amounts. The main drivers are:

  • Household income: parental or partner income sets the sliding scale — lower income means a higher loan, with an expectation that better-off families top up the rest.
  • Where you live: living with parents attracts the lowest rate — the Commons Library (April 2025) notes a maximum of £10,480 for students living with parents — while living away from home pays more and London pays most.
  • Course intensity: for 2025/26 the government sets part-time fee and maintenance bands by intensity, with a part-time fee level of £7,145 as a reference point — full-time students access the full maintenance scale.
  • Top-ups: bursaries, scholarships, Disabled Students' Allowance and NHS or teaching support sit outside the headline loan and can materially change the picture.

The controversial bit is the parental top-up assumption. Loughborough and HEPI analysis finds parents of students on the minimum loan would need to contribute around £13,865 a year — far beyond what many families can afford. When that contribution does not arrive, the £502 monthly gap lands on the student.

12 student saving tips that actually work

  1. Track every pound for 30 days. Use a free banking app to categorise spending; students typically find £40–£80 of forgotten subscriptions and food delivery.
  2. Shop the discounter ladder. Aldi and Lidl first, then reduced-to-clear bays after 7pm. Batch-cook two base meals (chilli, curry) weekly.
  3. Fix food waste. Meal-plan around one protein, freeze half, and run a "use-it-up" night. This alone can cut a £150 grocery bill by a quarter.
  4. Go bills-included where possible. A slightly higher rent with bills capped beats a cheap rent plus volatile energy bills — compare unit rates, not headlines.
  5. Split fairly, not equally. Use a bill-splitting app for energy, wifi and TV licence; assign the box room a lower share.
  6. Buy second-hand by default. Textbooks, kitchen kits, bikes and society gear from campus marketplaces cost 40–70% less than new.
  7. Stack student discounts. TOTUM, student railcard (saves a third on rail), and university gym off-peak memberships compound fast.
  8. Travel smart. Book trains 8–12 weeks ahead, shift to off-peak, and cycle or walk the under-two-mile trips that otherwise eat £20 a week.
  9. Cap the night out. Pre-drink responsibly at home, set a £20–£25 cash cap, and alternate paid socials with free society events.
  10. Build a £300 buffer. Even £10 a week into a separate pot stops the 34%-in-overdraft trap when a deposit or laptop repair hits.
  11. Sell and swap termly. End-of-term clear-outs of clothes, books and tickets fund next term's deposit and societies.
  12. Claim everything: council-tax student exemption, NHS low-income help with prescriptions and dental, and hardship funds below — unclaimed money is the most expensive kind.

Student part-time work UK: safe hours and smart jobs

Part-time work is now the norm: the Commons Library (April 2025) records the share of students working rising from 52% to 58% since 2014/15, with a median of 8 hours a week — but with stark gaps (Black British students averaging 12 hours, those from routine/manual backgrounds 14, and over-25s 20). Around 46% reported financial difficulties in 2021/22. LBC analysis in August 2025 suggested 20 hours may be needed for basic living on minimum support, while the Independent (August 2025) warned that heavy part-time work squeezes study, sport and societies and pushes more students to live at home.

  • Stay near 15 hours or under in term time unless your university advises otherwise; the 19-hour figure needed to fully close the gap is a red flag, not a target.
  • Prefer campus jobs: library, student ambassador, accommodation assistant and research-assistant shifts flex around exams and cut commuting.
  • Front-load hours in holidays: Christmas, Easter and summer full-time weeks build the buffer that protects term-time grades.
  • Check your contract: international students on a Student visa face weekly hour caps in term time — breaching them risks your visa.
  • Price the commute: a £12-an-hour job costing £6 and 90 minutes of travel is worse than a £10.50 campus shift next door.

Help funds, bursaries and extras most students miss

  • University hardship/help funds: cash grants for students in genuine need — ask student money advice, not just the general office.
  • Bursaries and scholarships: low-income, care-experienced, estranged-student and subject awards; many are automatic but some need a short form.
  • Part-time maintenance bands (2025/26): part-time students should check intensity-band eligibility rather than assuming no support.
  • Disabled Students' Allowance: equipment and mentoring that reduce study costs if you are eligible.
  • Emergency short-term loans: most universities offer interest-free bridging loans while funding applications land.
  • Course-specific pots: placement travel, background checks, studio materials and field trips often have dedicated departmental funds.
  • Charitable trusts: educational grant-search tools list small charities awarding a few hundred pounds for books and equipment; apply early before pots close.

How to ask: email student money advice with one page covering income, outgoings, the shortfall and what you have tried. Decisions are faster when you show a budget and evidence of the gap.

Final thoughts

A maintenance loan not being enough is a system-wide pattern in 2026, not a personal failure: £502 a month short, 58% working, and a £8,405 annual gap against the minimum income standard. Close it in layers — loan maximised, bills-included housing, 12 saving tips on repeat, term-time work held near 15 hours with holidays doing the heavy lifting, and every bursary and help fund claimed. If money is affecting your studies or health, talk to your student money advice team early; that is what they are for.
