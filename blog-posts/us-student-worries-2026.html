US Student Worries 2026: Costs & Safety

Affordability and gun violence top 2026 student worries. What Banjo, CIRCLE, UNICEF and Everytown found — and practical steps that help.

student worries 2026affordabilitycampus safetygun violence

Ask American students what keeps them up at night and two answers tower over the rest: affordability — the cost of living, housing, and college itself — and gun violence on campuses and in communities. The 2026 survey landscape is remarkably consistent: Banjo (2026) found cost of living the top issue for students (54% concerned, 41% naming it their single priority); CIRCLE/Tufts (2026) put cost of living, healthcare, and housing atop student concerns; UNICEF USA (2026) recorded gun violence among youths' highest worries; and Everytown (2026) documented how campus gun violence shapes daily student life. This guide maps what worries US students in 2026 — and what actually helps.

The through-line across every poll: students experience these as personal, daily pressures, not abstract politics. CIRCLE (2026) found nearly half (44%) of students sometimes or often struggle to meet basic financial needs, with 24% dissatisfied with their lives — and financial strain directly depresses civic participation and wellbeing.

Worry No. 1: Affordability

Banjo's April 2026 survey of 1,019 students across two- and four-year institutions is the clearest snapshot: cost of living/the economy ranks first (54% concerned; 41% top priority — far ahead of everything else), with healthcare, jobs, and housing rounding out the top four. Education affordability shows the widest intensity gap — flagged by 40% but named number one by only 9% — meaning it grinds constantly without always feeling like the crisis. CIRCLE (2026, n=5,549 ages 18–29) concurs: cost of living, healthcare, and housing dominate, with a third also citing jobs and education costs.

  • Housing first: the URGE/HIT (2026) youth-voter poll found 95% concerned about housing costs and 87% about affording basic needs; three in four struggle with bills or barely cover them.
  • College costs compound it: Study.com (2025) ranks finances/debt students' top challenge (50%), and Forbes Advisor (2026) finds 99% stressed about money.
  • What helps: file FAFSA yearly, appeal aid when income shifts, use campus food pantries and emergency grants without shame, work 10–15 hours in study-friendly jobs — our full debt survival plan systematises all of it.

Worry No. 2: Gun Violence

Safety fears run second only to money. UNICEF USA's 2026 Youth Poll places gun violence among the very top youth concerns (roughly three-quarters expressing worry), while Everytown Research (October 2026) reports campus gun violence cannot be solved by emergency preparedness alone — it requires strong gun-safety policies, early risk identification, expanded mental-health supports, trusted reporting channels, and community partnership. HBCU focus groups in the Everytown study describe delayed emergency notifications, inconsistent messaging, and the intertwining of campus safety with surrounding community violence, poverty, and disinvestment.

  • What students want: transparent, fast emergency communication; prevention-centred investment; and being treated as partners in safety planning, not just recipients of alerts.
  • What helps personally: save your campus alert system and crisis numbers now; know two evacuation routes from buildings you use weekly; use trusted reporting channels for concerning behaviour early.
  • What helps collectively: student-government safety audits, voter registration on gun-policy issues students care about, and peer-support networks that notice isolation before it hardens.

Beyond the Big Two: Healthcare, Jobs, Rights

Healthcare, jobs, and housing round out Banjo's top four; abortion/reproductive rights, education, and jobs each concern about a third (CIRCLE, 2026). Social justice, immigration, and climate register lower overall but with intensely committed minorities naming them number one. The practical takeaway: your worry profile is normal whatever its shape — and campus has an office for nearly every item on it (health centre, careers, advocacy, counselling).

Coping When the Worries Stack

  • Shrink one worry weekly: convert dread into a dated action — aid appeal filed, alert app installed, clinic booked. Progress beats reassurance.
  • Budget the controllables: food, transport, subscriptions — small wins fund the buffer that calms everything else.
  • Connect deliberately: one society, one shift community, one check-in friend. Belonging buffers both money stress and safety anxiety.
  • Limit doomscrolling: social feeds monetise fear — see our social-media guide for a healthier relationship with the feed.
  • Use campus help early: counselling, financial aid, dean-of-students emergency funds, and the 988 lifeline exist for exactly these pressures.

What Students Want From Campus Leaders

Surveys double as report cards for institutions. Across Banjo, CIRCLE, and Everytown (2026), the student wishlist is strikingly practical rather than ideological: transparent costs (no surprise fees; net-price calculators that tell the truth), fast emergency communication (alerts in minutes, honest follow-ups), visible basic-needs support (pantries, emergency grants, housing assistance advertised like sports teams are), and a seat at the table (students shaping safety plans and fee decisions, not just receiving them). Campuses that publish an annual "costs and safety" report in plain language earn disproportionate trust — secrecy is what converts worry into anger.

Build Your Personal Support Stack

No single office covers everything, so assemble yours before October midterms compress every queue:

  • Money corner: financial-aid adviser + one budgeting tool + emergency-grant application link saved.
  • Safety corner: campus alert app + two building exit routes memorised + trusted-reporting contact.
  • Mind corner: counselling centre number + 988 + one friend with a check-in pact.
  • Study corner: tutoring centre hours + one sprint routine + Academia Helper on WhatsApp for overload weeks.

Four corners, eight contacts, set up in a single afternoon — the cheapest insurance policy of your college career.

Turning Worry Into Action (and Votes)

Worry without agency curdles into cynicism — and 2026 data shows students refusing that trade. The URGE/HIT youth poll (2026) finds roughly half of young voters have boycotted a business, donated to a cause, or signed a petition, 47% have posted about an issue, and 29% have protested — with 70% saying mutual aid and collective action encourage them. The ladder works on campus too: start with one meeting (tenants' union, safety committee, food-security group), add one registration check (yours plus two friends'), then one sustained habit (monthly giving, meeting minutes, volunteer shift). CIRCLE (2026) shows informed students participate more — so the least glamorous action, reading the ballot guide, may matter most.

Worries are data: affordability says "plan me," safety says "prepare me." Answer both with one concrete action — and the nights get quieter.
