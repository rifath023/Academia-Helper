    How to Write Research Questions for a Dissertation

    Learn how to write strong research questions for a dissertation. Covers types of research questions, step-by-step development, FINER criteria, and examples across subjects.

    research questionsdissertationresearch designFINERacademic writingmethodology

    Your research question is the single most important sentence in your entire dissertation. Everything — your literature review, methodology, data collection, analysis, and conclusions — flows from it. A vague or poorly formed research question produces a confused dissertation. A sharp, well-formed one gives your entire project clarity and direction.

    This guide walks you through exactly how to write research questions for a dissertation, with examples across disciplines and a step-by-step development process. For expert dissertation writing support, Academia Helper is here to help.

    Quick Summary: A strong dissertation research question is specific, focused, researchable, original, and directly linked to a gap in the existing literature. It should be answerable within your timeframe and scope. Most dissertations have one main research question supported by 2–4 sub-questions.

    What Is a Research Question?

    A research question is the central inquiry your dissertation sets out to answer. Unlike a thesis statement (which makes a claim), a research question opens an inquiry. It defines the scope of your study, guides your literature review, and determines your methodology.

    ❌ Weak Research Questions

    • "What is leadership?" (too broad, already answered)
    • "Does social media affect people?" (vague, unanswerable)
    • "Is climate change bad?" (not academic, not open)
    • "How can companies improve?" (too general)

    ✅ Strong Research Questions

    • Specific and focused
    • Answerable with available methods
    • Linked to a genuine gap in literature
    • Original — not already definitively answered
    • Feasible within your timeframe and resources

    Types of Research Questions

    Descriptive

    Descriptive

    Describes a phenomenon as it is. "What are the current social media practices of UK SMEs?" Answered through surveys, observation, or content analysis.

    Comparative

    Comparative

    Compares two or more groups or phenomena. "How do leadership styles differ between UK and German manufacturing firms?" Uses comparative analysis.

    Causal

    Causal / Explanatory

    Examines cause-and-effect relationships. "To what extent does transformational leadership predict employee retention?" Uses quantitative or experimental methods.

    Exploratory

    Exploratory

    Explores an under-researched phenomenon. "How do first-generation university students experience imposter syndrome?" Uses qualitative methods.

    Evaluative

    Evaluative

    Assesses the effectiveness of an intervention or programme. "How effective is mindfulness training in reducing burnout among NHS nurses?" Common in health and education research.

    Interpretive

    Interpretive

    Seeks to understand meaning or experience. "What meanings do female entrepreneurs attach to business failure?" Typically qualitative and phenomenological.

    How to Develop Your Research Question: Step by Step

    1

    Choose a broad topic area

    Start with a general subject area you are interested in and that aligns with your course. Example: employee wellbeing in remote working environments.

    2

    Read around the topic

    Conduct a preliminary literature search to understand what is already known, what is debated, and where gaps exist. Your research question should address a gap.

    3

    Identify a specific gap or problem

    Example gap: "Most studies on remote work wellbeing focus on the USA. Little is known about UK-specific contexts, particularly in SMEs."

    4

    Draft an initial question

    Turn the gap into a question: "How does remote working affect the psychological wellbeing of employees in UK SMEs?"

    5

    Refine using the FINER criteria

    Check your question is: Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant. Refine until all five criteria are met.

    6

    Add sub-questions

    Break the main question into 2–4 sub-questions that structure your chapters. Example: (1) What wellbeing challenges do remote workers in UK SMEs report? (2) What coping strategies do they employ? (3) What role does managerial support play?

    Common Research Question Mistakes

    • Too broad — cannot be answered within a dissertation scope
    • Too narrow — not enough literature or data available
    • Already definitively answered in existing literature
    • Not linked to a gap identified in the literature review
    • Answerable with a simple yes/no rather than analysis
    • Multiple unconnected questions with no unifying focus

    Final Thoughts

    Your research question is the foundation everything else is built on. Taking the time to develop it carefully — through reading, gap identification, and iterative refinement — pays dividends across the entire dissertation.     Frequently Asked Questions

    How many research questions should a dissertation have?
    Most dissertations have one main research question supported by 2–4 sub-questions. The sub-questions break the main question into manageable parts, each typically addressed by a separate section of your findings and discussion chapters.
    What is the FINER criteria for research questions?
    FINER stands for Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, and Relevant. It is a checklist used to evaluate whether a research question is suitable for a dissertation or research study. A question that meets all five criteria is well-positioned for academic research.
    What is the difference between a research question and a hypothesis?
    A research question is an open inquiry that your study sets out to answer. A hypothesis is a specific, testable prediction about the expected answer. Quantitative studies often use hypotheses; qualitative studies typically use research questions only.
    How do I find a gap in the literature for my research question?
    Read broadly around your topic in academic databases. Look for what has NOT been studied — specific populations, geographies, time periods, or contexts that existing research has overlooked. Your supervisor is also an excellent source of guidance on current gaps in your field.
