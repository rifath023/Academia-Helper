Dissertation Discussion Chapter Examples: How to Link Findings to Literature (Without Repeating Results)

Your examiners do not want your results again in new words. They want interpretation. These dissertation discussion chapter examples show exactly how to link findings to literature, explain surprises, and earn analysis marks.

dissertation discussion link findings literature discussion chapter example UK interpret findings vs results academic implications discussion

If you are writing a Masters dissertation and staring at a discussion chapter example UK wondering why yours sounds like a repeat of Chapter 4, you are not alone. The single most common examiner comment in the UK is: “Descriptive — restates results without linking to literature.”

This guide fixes that. You will learn how to dissertation discussion link findings literature properly: summarise the answer to each research question, compare and contrast with 2–3 key studies, explain why you agree or differ, and draw out theoretical and practical implications. We include two full copy-adaptable example paragraphs, a results vs discussion table, and a UK examiner checklist. For the full chapter structure, start with our how to write a dissertation discussion chapter guide.

Results vs Discussion: Stop Repeating, Start Interpreting

Understanding interpret findings vs results is the threshold skill. Results say what you found. Discussion says what it means, why it matters, and how it changes what we knew. If you can move a sentence from one chapter to the other without changing it, it is in the wrong place.

FeatureResults Chapter (Ch. 4)Discussion Chapter (Ch. 5)
PurposeReport objectively what the data showInterpret, compare to literature, explain why
Tables / quotesFull tables, statistics, verbatim quotesNo new tables; refer back: “As shown in Table 4.2…”
CitationsMinimal (methods only)Dense — every key claim linked to literature
Language“The mean was 4.12 (SD = 0.71); 68% agreed…”“This high score confirms… in contrast to… suggesting…”
Example — quantitative“H1 was supported (β = .42, p < .001).”“The strong effect for H1 extends Deci and Ryan (2000) by showing autonomy matters even in hybrid NHS teams, unlike Brown (2022) where…”
Example — qualitativeTheme 1: “Flexible distrust” with 3 quotes“‘Flexible distrust’ challenges Allen et al. (2015) by revealing that…”
Examiner red flagInterpreting too earlyRepeating p-values / quotes without analysis

Golden rule: in the discussion, cite the table once, then never mention the number again. One sentence of “what” → three to four sentences of “so what and says who.” That ratio is what moves you from descriptive to critical writing — see our guide to descriptive to critical writing.

Weak: “Table 4.3 shows 72% of respondents agreed flexible working improves satisfaction (M = 4.12). This is a high percentage.” — Strong: “The finding that flexible working predicts satisfaction answers RQ1 and confirms Gajendran and Harrison (2007), while qualifying Smith (2023) whose post-pandemic sample found…”

The 4-Move Framework Examiners Reward

UK markers look for “discussion moves” — the rhetorical steps that turn data into argument. Use this 4-move paragraph framework for every key finding. It works for surveys, interviews, and secondary dissertations.

Move 1 — Answer: State the answer to the RQ / hypothesis in one sentence. No numbers dump.

Move 2 — Compare / Contrast: Link to 2–3 named studies from your literature review. Use agree / partially agree / contradict language with citations.

Move 3 — Explain Why: Offer a theoretically or contextually grounded explanation. Reference theory, sample, setting, method, or time period. This is where criticality lives.

Move 4 — Implicate: State what this means for theory, practice, or policy in one sentence. Save the full implications section for later, but signpost now.

Order your discussion by research questions, not by the order of your questionnaire or themes. Start each subsection with the RQ as a heading: “5.2 Discussion of RQ1: To what extent does flexible working predict job satisfaction?” Then apply Moves 1–4. Repeat for RQ2, RQ3. End with a synthesis paragraph that answers your overall aim.

UK expectations add two constraints: (1) every major claim needs a citation — unsupported opinion fails; (2) you must show awareness of limitations as you argue, not only in a bolt-on paragraph. Phrases like “within this London-only, cross-sectional sample…” signal evaluative judgement and protect you at viva.

Example Paragraph 1: When Your Finding AGREES with Literature

Use this pattern when your data confirm prior work. Your job is not to say “this proves Smith was right” but to show how you extend, confirm in a new context, or strengthen the claim. Note the sentence-by-sentence moves in brackets — remove them in your final draft.

Copy-Adapt Example — Agree + Extend (MSc HRM, quantitative)

“In response to RQ1, this study found that perceived autonomy is the strongest predictor of job satisfaction among hybrid NHS administrative staff [Move 1 — Answer]. This confirms Deci and Ryan’s (2000) self-determination theory and aligns directly with Gajendran and Harrison’s (2007) meta-analysis, which reported a positive effect of telecommuting on autonomy and satisfaction across 46 studies, as well as recent UK evidence from Jones and Taylor (2023) in NHS community teams [Move 2 — Compare]. The slightly larger effect observed here (β = .42) can plausibly be explained by the post-pandemic hybrid context: unlike pre-2020 samples where flexible working was voluntary and rare, participants in this study had two years of normalised hybrid practice and supportive line management, which amplifies the autonomy mechanism central to SDT [Move 3 — Explain why]. This suggests the autonomy–satisfaction link is not only robust but strengthened when hybrid work is institutionally embedded rather than idiosyncratic, extending Gajendran and Harrison (2007) to a mature-hybrid NHS setting [Move 4 — Implicate].”

Why it scores: names the theory, cites the meta-analysis plus a recent UK study, explains the stronger effect through context rather than ignoring it, and claims a modest extension — exactly what Merit/Distinction descriptors ask for.

Adapt it by swapping the finding, the two anchor citations from your own literature review, and the explanatory mechanism (culture, sector, method, period). Keep the verbs precise: confirms, corroborates, replicates in a […] context, extends […] by showing […]. Avoid “proves” — quantitative examiners penalise it.

Example Paragraph 2: When Your Finding CONTRADICTS Literature

Contradictions earn the highest marks if you explain them rather than hide them. Never write “this may be due to small sample size” and stop — that is a limitation, not an explanation. Offer a substantive theoretical, contextual, or methodological reason, then acknowledge the limitation honestly.

Copy-Adapt Example — Contradict + Explain (MSc Education, qualitative)

“In relation to RQ2, teachers in this study described performance-related pay as undermining collaboration, directly contradicting Brown and Leigh (2021), who found a positive effect on motivation among academy teachers in the North East, and partially qualifying Thorpe (2022), who reported mixed effects [Move 1 + 2 — Answer and contrast]. This divergence can be explained in two ways. First, participants taught in small, high-deprivation London primaries where teamwork is essential for covering staff absence — a context in which competitive incentives plausibly crowd out intrinsic motivation, consistent with Frey and Jegen’s (2001) motivation crowding theory [Move 3a — Theoretical explanation]. Second, Brown and Leigh (2021) used a survey measuring individual self-reported effort, whereas this study’s semi-structured interviews captured collective norms and informal sanctions against ‘showing off’, which surveys under-detect [Move 3b — Methodological explanation]. While the small, purposive sample (n = 12) limits generalisability, the consistency of this theme across all three schools suggests the contradiction is substantive rather than artefactual, and indicates that PRP effects are contingent on school context rather than universal [Move 4 — Implicate + qualify].”

Why it scores: names who is contradicted, offers two distinct explanations (theory + method), and turns a small sample from a weakness into a reasoned qualifier. That is critical analysis.

Useful contradicting phrases: in contrast to, challenges, fails to replicate, qualifies, suggests a boundary condition on, indicates […] is context-dependent. Always pair a contradiction with at least one citation that helps explain it — a theory, a contextual study, or a methods paper (e.g., on what interviews reveal vs surveys).

Academic Implications: Split Theory vs Practice (Examiners Check This)

Many students write one vague “implications” paragraph. UK marking schemes split academic implications discussion into theoretical contribution and practical / policy implications. Write them as two labelled subsections — it forces precision and makes marking easier.

TypeWhat to claimSentence starterExample
TheoreticalConfirm, extend, refine, or bound a theory / model“This study extends […] by showing […] in a […] context…”“Extends SDT by showing autonomy effects strengthen under institutionalised hybrid work.”
Practical — managerialWhat managers / teachers / clinicians should do differently“For practice, the findings suggest […] should prioritise…”“NHS line managers should co-design hybrid rotas to protect autonomy rather than monitor hours.”
PolicyWhat policy / guidance should change“At policy level, […] indicates a need to…”“DfE guidance on PRP should allow exemption for small high-deprivation primaries.”
MethodologicalWhat future designs should do (only if genuine)“Methodologically, the divergence between survey and interview evidence suggests…”“Future PRP studies should combine surveys with observation of team norms.”

Be modest and specific. A Masters dissertation does not “fill a gap in the literature” — it “provides additional UK evidence on […] / offers a boundary condition on […] / replicates […] in a new setting.” Overclaiming (“this proves the theory wrong”) is a fast route to a capped mark. Link each implication back to a finding you actually discussed; never introduce a new recommendation with no evidential base.

Close the implications with a bridge to your conclusion: “These implications are summarised and carried forward into actionable recommendations in Chapter 6.” For how to do that without repeating yourself, see how to write a dissertation conclusion.

Limitations Without Sabotaging Yourself + Future Research

Examiners want critical honesty, not apology. State each limitation in one sentence, say what it affects, and say what you did to mitigate it. Then turn it into a concrete future-research suggestion. Never list limitations you could have fixed easily (e.g., “no proofreading due to time”).

  • ✅ Sample: “The purposive sample of 12 London teachers limits statistical generalisability; transferability is supported through thick description, and future research should replicate in rural and secondary settings.”
  • ✅ Design: “The cross-sectional design captures perceptions at one point; a longitudinal design could test whether autonomy effects persist beyond the first year of hybrid work.”
  • ✅ Method / bias: “Self-report questionnaires risk social desirability bias; anonymity and validated scales (α = .83) were used to mitigate this, and future studies could triangulate with manager ratings.”
  • ✅ Scope: “Focusing on NHS administrative staff delimits the study to one trust; comparative work in private-sector services would test boundary conditions.”
  • ✅ Analysis: “Single-researcher coding may introduce interpretive bias; an audit trail and supervisor review of 20% of transcripts were used, and independent double-coding is recommended next.”

Place limitations after implications so you end your argument on contribution, not apology. Write 200–300 words maximum — a full page of limitations signals a failed design. Then give 2–3 specific future directions, each linked to a limitation above, not a generic “more research is needed.”

UK Examiner Checklist: Does Your Discussion Pass?

Run this check before submission. If you tick all ten, you are writing at Merit–Distinction level for analysis and evaluation.

  • ✅ Discussion is organised by RQ / hypothesis, not by questionnaire order
  • ✅ Each key finding summarised in 1 sentence — no re-pasted tables or long quotes
  • ✅ Every key finding compared to 2–3 named studies from the literature review
  • ✅ Agreement and disagreement explicitly labelled (confirms / contradicts / qualifies)
  • ✅ At least one theoretical or contextual explanation for each major finding
  • ✅ Unexpected findings explained, not buried
  • ✅ Separate theoretical vs practical / policy implications
  • ✅ Limitations stated with mitigation + linked future research (not apologies)
  • ✅ Critical language throughout — evaluates, weighs, justifies (not describes)
  • ✅ Final synthesis paragraph answers the overall aim and bridges to conclusion

If you are missing Moves 3 and 4 across most paragraphs, that is why your draft feels repetitive. Add one “because” sentence (theory/context/method) and one “so what” sentence (implication) to each — word count rises, but analysis marks rise faster.

Frequently Asked Questions

Say whether you agree, partially agree or contradict, explain why using theory or context, and state what this means for theory or practice. Never paste tables or p-values again — interpret them.

Results report what you found objectively with tables and themes and no interpretation. Discussion interprets what those findings mean, compares them to existing literature, explains surprises, and draws theoretical and practical implications.

For a 12,000–15,000 word Masters dissertation, aim for 2,000–3,000 words for a standalone discussion, or 3,000–4,000 words for a combined Discussion and Conclusion. For a 10,000-word undergraduate dissertation, 1,500–2,000 words is typical. Always follow your handbook.

Final Thoughts

A strong discussion chapter example UK examiners reward does four things in every paragraph: answers the question, compares to named literature, explains why with theory or context, and states the implication. Master interpret findings vs results — one sentence of “what,” three sentences of “so what” — split your academic implications discussion into theory and practice, own your limitations in a sentence each, and close by answering your overall aim. Do that, and your discussion stops repeating Chapter 4 and starts earning analysis marks.
