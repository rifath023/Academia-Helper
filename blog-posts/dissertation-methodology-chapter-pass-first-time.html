How to Write a Dissertation Methodology Chapter That Passes First Time (UK Structure)

If you want to know how to write dissertation methodology chapter that actually passes, start here. UK examiners fail this chapter more than any other — not for bad English, but for missing justification. This guide gives you the exact structure, word counts, philosophy shortcut, and examples.

how to write dissertation methodology chapter dissertation methodology structure UK methodology chapter example

Learning how to write dissertation methodology chapter correctly is the difference between a comfortable merit and a stressful resubmission. In UK universities — from Manchester to Leeds, UCL to Glasgow — the methodology chapter (usually Chapter 3) is where examiners test one thing: can you justify every choice you made?

Most students describe what they did. Passing students explain why it was the most appropriate way to answer their research questions, what they rejected, and what the limitations are. This article shows you the full dissertation methodology structure UK supervisors expect, with word counts, a one-paragraph philosophy trick based on Saunders’ Research Onion, quant vs qual guidance, sampling and ethics checklists, and sentence starters you can use today.

Golden rule from Westminster, Sunderland and most UK handbooks: if your department gave you a structure, follow it exactly. If not, use the 8-section structure below — it aligns with Saunders et al. (2019) and Creswell expectations.

Why Do Dissertation Methodology Chapters Fail in the UK?

External examiners report the same five failures every year:

  1. No alignment: philosophy says interpretivism, but method is a positivist survey with no explanation. The Research Onion layers contradict each other.
  2. Description without justification: “I used purposive sampling of 12 participants” — but why 12, why purposive, why not random? No citation.
  3. Missing ethics and GDPR: no mention of consent, anonymity, data storage, right to withdraw, or university ethics approval. Automatic referral at many UK universities.
  4. Vague analysis: “Data was analysed using thematic analysis” — with no steps, no Braun & Clarke (2006) reference, no software, no example of coding. Or “SPSS was used” with no tests named.
  5. No limitations or reliability discussion: pretending the study is perfect signals lack of critical evaluation. Examiners want delimitations, limitations, and validity or trustworthiness.

The fix is simple: follow a predictable structure, justify each layer, and close the loop from question → design → evidence. The rest of this guide does exactly that.

What Is the Dissertation Methodology Structure UK Examiners Expect?

The standard dissertation methodology structure UK has eight sections. Use these as sub-headings (3.1, 3.2, etc.) and allocate words as shown for a 2,000-word Masters chapter:

#SectionWordsWhat to Cover
3.1Introduction & research design summary150–200Restate aim/questions, map chapter, state design in one line
3.2Research philosophy & approach250–300Positivism / interpretivism / pragmatism; deductive / inductive / abductive; Research Onion link
3.3Methodological choice & research design250–300Quantitative survey, qualitative, mixed; strategy: survey, case study, interviews; time horizon
3.4Sampling strategy250–300Population, technique, size justification, inclusion/exclusion
3.5Data collection methods & instruments300–400Questionnaire design / interview guide, pilot, procedure
3.6Data analysis250–350SPSS tests or thematic analysis steps, validity/trustworthiness
3.7Ethics, GDPR & limitations250–300Consent, anonymity, storage, ethics approval, limitations/delimitations
3.8Chapter summary100–150Bridge to findings: how this design answers your questions

For secondary dissertations, replace 3.4–3.5 with search strategy, inclusion criteria (PRISMA-style), databases, and quality appraisal. Keep the same logic: what you did, why, and what you excluded.

How Do You Write Research Philosophy in One Paragraph?

UK students overthink philosophy. You do not need three pages on ontology and epistemology. Examiners want to see you understand Saunders’ Research Onion (Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill, 2019) and can peel it coherently: philosophy → approach to theory development → methodological choice → strategy → time horizon → techniques.

The six layers in order are: (1) philosophy — positivism, critical realism, interpretivism, postmodernism, pragmatism; (2) approach — deduction, induction, abduction; (3) methodological choice — mono-method quantitative/qualitative, multi-method, mixed-method simple/complex; (4) strategy — survey, case study, ethnography, grounded theory, narrative inquiry, action research, archival; (5) time horizon — cross-sectional vs longitudinal; (6) techniques — data collection and analysis.

The 5-sentence philosophy trick

Use this formula and cite Saunders et al. (2019) plus Creswell and Creswell (2018) for designs:

  1. State philosophy + definition in your words.
  2. Link it to your research aim.
  3. State approach (deductive/inductive).
  4. State choice + strategy + time horizon.
  5. Justify why alternatives were rejected.

Quantitative survey methodology chapter example (positivism, 98 words):

“This study adopts a positivist philosophy, which assumes an objective reality that can be measured through structured observation (Saunders et al., 2019). As the aim is to test the relationship between flexible working and job satisfaction among NHS nurses, a deductive approach was adopted to test hypotheses derived from the literature. A mono-method quantitative choice was therefore appropriate, using a cross-sectional survey strategy to collect standardised data at one point in time. An interpretivist interview design was rejected because the study seeks generalisable patterns rather than in-depth meanings.”

Qualitative example (interpretivism): “This study adopts interpretivism, which holds that reality is socially constructed and best understood through participants’ lived experience. Consistent with an inductive approach, semi-structured interviews were used to build themes rather than test theory, using a cross-sectional case-study strategy in two London primary schools.”

Pragmatism is ideal for mixed methods: “what works to answer the question.” Critical realism suits MBA Studies where mechanisms matter. Pick one, stay consistent, and never mix positivist language (“prove causality”) with a 8-person interview sample.

If you are doing a quantitative survey methodology chapter, define your variables early — independent, dependent, control — and state hypotheses. If qualitative, define your tradition (case study, phenomenology, grounded theory) in two sentences. Do not claim mixed methods if you only added two open questions to a survey; examiners call that a mono-method survey with open comments.

How Do You Justify Sampling Like a First-Class Student?

Sampling is where most referrals happen. You need four facts plus justification: population, technique, size, and inclusion/exclusion criteria.

UK examiners expect you to distinguish probability (random, stratified) from non-probability (purposive, convenience, snowball). Most undergrad/Masters primary studies use non-probability for access reasons — that is acceptable if you own it. Read our full comparison: convenience vs purposive sampling.

Sampling justification example (MSc HRM, purposive):

“The population was line managers in three Manchester retail firms (approx. N=120). Purposive sampling was used to select 14 managers with at least two years’ experience of hybrid-team supervision, as they could provide information-rich accounts relevant to the research questions (Etikan et al., 2016). Inclusion criteria were: (1) current line-management responsibility, (2) experience of appraisal post-2023. Convenience sampling alone was rejected because it would include staff without relevant experience and weaken credibility. While findings are not statistically generalisable, transferability is supported through thick description of context.”

For surveys, justify size with a rule: Krejcie & Morgan table, G*Power, or prior similar UK studies + response-rate allowance. For interviews, cite saturation (Guest et al., 2006: 12 interviews often sufficient) and stop when no new themes emerge. Always report response rate, non-response, and how you accessed participants (gatekeeper letter, LinkedIn, university pool).

How Do You Write Data Collection and Data Analysis?

Quantitative: questionnaire to SPSS

State: instrument source (adapted/adopted with citation), scale types, pilot (n=10–15), distribution (Jisc Online Surveys / Qualtrics, dates), and response handling. Link each section of the questionnaire to a research objective in a mapping table — examiners love this.

Then name exact analyses: descriptives (mean, SD, frequencies), reliability (Cronbach’s alpha ≥0.70), normality (Shapiro-Wilk), and inferential tests (chi-square, t-test, ANOVA, Pearson/Spearman correlation, multiple regression). Mention SPSS version and significance level (p < 0.05). See how to clean survey data in SPSS before any test, and handle missing data explicitly.

Template sentence: “Section B measured job satisfaction using 8 items adapted from Spector (1997) on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The questionnaire was piloted with 12 MSc peers; ambiguous wording in Q7 was revised, yielding Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83 in the main study.”

Qualitative: interviews to themes

State: interview type and why, interview guide development (literature + RQs, 8–12 open questions, probes), duration (30–60 min), recording (Teams/Zoom, Otter transcription, manual check), and setting. Then detail Braun & Clarke’s six phases: familiarisation, coding, theme searching, reviewing, defining, writing up — plus software (NVivo 14 or manual) and an audit trail.

Address qualitative methodology trustworthiness using Lincoln and Guba (1985): credibility (member checking, triangulation), transferability (thick description), dependability (audit trail, supervisor review), confirmability (reflexive journal). What Must UK Ethics and GDPR Cover to Avoid Referral?

UK universities require faculty ethics approval before data collection. State your approval reference and date. Then cover each point — one sentence each is enough:

  • ✅ Informed consent: participant information sheet + signed consent form, voluntary participation, right to withdraw up to [date] without reason.
  • ✅ Anonymity vs confidentiality: pseudonyms (P1, P2), removal of identifiers, no named firms without written permission.
  • ✅ GDPR & Data Protection Act 2018: lawful basis (consent / public task), data minimisation, storage on password-protected university OneDrive, retention (e.g., destroyed 12 months after award), participants’ rights to access/erase.
  • ✅ Safeguarding & sensitive topics: distress protocol, signposting, DBS if working with children/vulnerable adults, no deception.
  • ✅ Secondary data: Harvard referencing, no plagiarism, copyright compliance for scales/figures.

Example ethics paragraph: “Ethical approval was granted by the University of Leeds Business School Ethics Committee (Ref: LUBS-2025-184, 12 March 2026). All participants received an information sheet and provided written consent. Data were pseudonymised, stored on encrypted university storage in compliance with GDPR, and will be deleted 12 months post-award. Participants could withdraw until 30 April 2026.”

Finish with limitations and delimitations. Limitation = outside your control (small sample, cross-sectional design, self-report bias, London-only). Delimitation = your boundary choice (MSc nurses only, 2024–2026 data). Never hide them — critical awareness earns marks.

Which Sentence Starters Make Methodology Writing Easier?

Copy-adapt these high-scoring openers — they force justification:

  • “This chapter outlines and justifies the methodological choices adopted to address [RQ1–RQ3]…”
  • “Guided by Saunders et al.’s (2019) Research Onion, this study adopts…”
  • “A [deductive/inductive] approach was deemed most appropriate because…”
  • “[Survey / case study / semi-structured interviews] was selected over [alternative] because…”
  • “Purposive sampling was justified on the grounds that…”
  • “Reliability was assessed via Cronbach’s alpha, while construct validity was supported by…” / “Trustworthiness was enhanced through member checking, triangulation and a reflexive journal…”
  • “A key limitation is…, which affects generalisability/transferability. This was mitigated by…”

Write in past tense for what you did, present tense for principles. Cite methods literature — Saunders, Creswell, Bryman, Field (SPSS), Braun & Clarke — at least 8–12 references in this chapter alone.

Final Thoughts: Write to Justify, Not Just Describe

A first-time pass comes from one habit: every choice gets a reason, a citation, and a rejected alternative. Use the 8-section dissertation methodology structure UK above, keep philosophy to one sharp paragraph anchored in Saunders’ Research Onion, justify sampling with population + criteria + size, name your SPSS tests or thematic steps, and close with ethics, GDPR and honest limitations.

If you do that, your examiner can trace a clean line from research questions to evidence — and has no reason to refer you. And if you are short on time, get a second pair of expert eyes before you submit.
