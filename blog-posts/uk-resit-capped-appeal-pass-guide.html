Resit Capped at 40%? How UK Resits Work + How to Appeal and Pass Next Time

Failed a module and just discovered your resit is capped at 40%? You are not alone — and it is not the end of your degree. Here is exactly why UK resits are capped, how to appeal for uncapped marks, and how to pass next time.

why resit capped at 40 UK UK resit capped marks appeal resit vs resubmission UK how to pass resit UK

Seeing “resit – mark capped at 40%” on your results letter feels brutal. You failed one essay or exam, you are already stressed about academic probation or progression, and now the university tells you that even if you write a brilliant resubmission, the highest mark you can keep is a bare pass.

Take a breath. A capped resit is normal in the UK, it does not mean you are a bad student, and it does not automatically ruin your degree. In this guide we explain why your resit is capped at 40 in the UK, what a capped resit assessment actually does to your transcript, the difference between resit vs resubmission vs retake, how to appeal a resit for uncapped marks, and a practical 4-week plan to pass your resit next time.

Quick answer: most UK undergraduate resits are capped at 40% because 40% is the pass mark. You must still do pass-standard work — ideally 55–65% in real terms to be safe — but your transcript will record 40%. The only standard way to remove the cap is an upheld mitigating or extenuating circumstances claim. Always check your course handbook, because professional courses, foundation years and postgraduate taught degrees use different rules.

What Does “Capped at 40%” Actually Mean?

A cap limits the module mark recorded on your transcript and used for progression and classification, not the academic judgement of your work. Your tutor still marks your resit out of 100 using the normal criteria. If you produce 2:1-level work, you will see kind feedback — but the exam board will then apply the cap.

For undergraduates (Levels 4–6), the cap is almost always 40. For postgraduate taught (Level 7), it is normally 50. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland follow the same convention. Some universities show both marks — for example LSE records the actual resit mark alongside a Pass grade — but for award calculations only 40 counts.

Two details students often miss:

  • You cannot “bank” extra marks. A 70% resit does not give you 70% for classification, and it does not offset another failed module. Read our full explainer on capped resit marks in the UK.
  • Best attempt does not always stand. Some universities (for example UHI, York St John) keep the higher of first and resit component marks if you still pass overall; others replace everything with the resit. Check whether you must redo all components or only failed ones.

Why Is My Resit Capped at 40 in the UK?

Universities cap resits for three consistent reasons across the sector in 2026:

1. Fairness to first-attempt passes

You have already had a full semester of teaching, library access, formative feedback and one marked attempt. Allowing unlimited uncapped second attempts would disadvantage students who passed first time under exam pressure. The cap preserves the value of a first sit.

2. Academic standards and professional credibility

External examiners and professional bodies (NMC, ACCA, BPS, Engineering Council and others) expect second attempts to demonstrate threshold competence, not to improve classification. Capping signals that you met the learning outcomes at the second opportunity.

3. Progression, not perfection

UK regulations frame resits as a retrieval opportunity. The goal is to let you progress with 120 credits, not to rewrite your average. That is why resits are usually free, held in the late-summer resit period, and without extra tuition — unlike a paid repeat year. UCL, Kent, UWE Bristol, Loughborough and Essex all publish this distinction explicitly.

Does that feel harsh when you were 2 marks off, or ill, or hit by a late submission penalty that tipped you into failure? Yes. That is precisely why every university has a mitigating circumstances route to an uncapped attempt — see the appeal section below.

Resit vs Resubmission vs Retake (Repeat): What Is the Difference?

Students use these words interchangeably, but in UK regulations they mean very different things for fees, attendance, visas and capping. Our detailed comparison is here: resit vs resubmission UK. Summary:

Visa impactOut-of-attendance resits can lead to sponsorship withdrawal — you may need to leave and returnSame as resitIn-attendance repeats maintain sponsorship for taught terms

Examples: Kent defines resitting as retaking an exam and resubmitting as redoing coursework, both without tuition; repeating means attending everything again and paying. UWE Bristol charges nothing for resits but charges for retakes and wipes previous marks. Loughborough calls an uncapped second chance after valid mitigation a “Repeat First Attempt” and a still-capped second chance a “Repeat Second Attempt”.

Which one will you be offered? If you failed under 60 credits, most boards offer a capped summer resit/resubmission. If you failed more than 60 credits or failed your resit, you will typically be offered a part-time or full-time repeat, trailing, or withdrawal. International students should always check with immigration advice before accepting an out-of-attendance resit.

How to Appeal a Capped Resit Mark in the UK

You cannot appeal because you think 40% is unfair or because you disagree with academic judgement. Every UK university — and the Office of the Independent Adjudicator (OIA) — rejects those appeals. You can appeal on three recognised grounds:

  1. Mitigating / extenuating circumstances you could not disclose in time — illness, bereavement, assault, serious mental-health crisis, significant technical failure during an online exam, or other events beyond your control that materially affected performance.
  2. Procedural irregularity — the board misapplied regulations, lost work, miscalculated marks, failed to implement approved adjustments, or did not consider your approved mitigation.
  3. Bias or prejudice — rare, but valid where you can evidence that you were marked differently for an improper reason.

Step 1: Act within 10–15 working days

Most deadlines are 10–15 working days from publication of ratified results (DMU: 15 working days; Surrey, Birkbeck, Exeter: similar windows). Mitigating circumstances claims themselves must usually be filed before the board meets — often within 7 days of the assessment. Once you have sat a capped resit, universities including UEL and LSE state the cap cannot then be retrospectively removed. Speed matters.

Step 2: Gather independent evidence

Tribunals require third-party, dated evidence, not self-statements alone:

  • GP letter, hospital discharge note, counsellor letter on headed paper with dates covering the assessment period
  • Death certificate or order of service for bereavement
  • Police report, student support or wellbeing referral records
  • Screenshots with timestamps plus IT service ticket for technical failures
  • Certified translation if evidence is not in English

Explain good reason for late disclosure: why you could not or for valid reason did not tell the university earlier. “I was embarrassed” alone is rarely enough without clinical support; “I was hospitalised and attach discharge papers” is.

Step 3: Write a concise appeal letter

Use this outline (one to two pages plus appendices):

  • Header: name, ID, programme, module codes, date of ratified result, appeal deadline
  • Ground: state which ground (e.g. “unreported mitigating circumstances + good reason”)
  • Chronology: bullet timeline of what happened and when
  • Impact: link each event to the specific assessment (“panic attack night before 12 May exam; could not revise or sleep”)
  • Good reason for delay: why you did not submit mitigation earlier
  • Remedy sought: e.g. “discount first attempt and grant uncapped first sit / deferral” — note marks cannot simply be increased
  • Evidence list: numbered appendices referenced in text

Keep tone factual and respectful. Get your students’ union advice centre to review it before submission — they do this daily and know your university’s form.

Step 4: What happens next and the OIA

The university conducts an initial eligibility check (usually within 10 days), then a faculty panel or assessment board reviews the case (often within 30 calendar days). Possible outcomes: reject, uphold with uncapped first sit/deferral, or offer a repeat second attempt (still capped). If rejected and you have exhausted internal procedures, request a Completion of Procedures letter (issued within 28 days) and complain to the Office of the Independent Adjudicator for Higher Education (OIAHE) within 12 months. The OIA reviews process fairness, not academic judgement, and can recommend uncapped resits or compensation where the process was flawed.

Important: This is general guidance on 2026 UK norms synthesised from published regulations (LSE, UCL, Kent, UWE, Loughborough, Essex, KCL, QMUL, DMU, Surrey, Birkbeck and others). It is not legal advice. Capping, condonement, compensation and appeal windows vary by institution, level and professional-body rules — always check your student handbook and academic regulations before acting.

How to Pass Your Resit Next Time: A 4-Week Plan

Resits fail for predictable reasons: repeating the same essay with light edits, revising everything instead of what was examined, or aiming for “just 40%” and falling short. Examiners still apply full standards — you need genuine pass-plus work. Here is a focused plan that works for both exams and resubmissions.

Week 1: Diagnose (Days 1–7)

  • Download your feedback sheet, marking rubric and module handbook. Highlight every criterion where you scored below pass.
  • Email your tutor one polite, specific question: “Could you clarify which two improvements would most help my resubmission pass?” Do not ask for pre-marking.
  • For exams, collect past papers and model answers; log which topics and question types lost you marks.
  • Confirm logistics: resit date, format (in-person/online), word count, referencing style, and whether you redo all components or only failed ones.

Week 2: Rebuild (Days 8–14)

  • Create a new outline from scratch. Do not patch the failed essay — restructure around the question and rubric.
  • For each section, write a topic sentence that directly answers the brief, then one piece of theory plus one applied example with citation.
  • Fix academic skills that commonly cause fails: Harvard/APA referencing, critical analysis vs description, paragraph structure, and proofreading. If referencing sank you last time, see our guide to late and academic penalties for how small technical deductions compound.
  • Exam students: make condensed one-page topic sheets and do open-book timed drills.

Week 3: Practise under conditions (Days 15–21)

  • Coursework: complete a full draft 7 days before the deadline. Run it through your university’s similarity checker and read it aloud.
  • Exams: sit at least three full past papers under strict timed, closed-book conditions. Mark harshly against grade descriptors.
  • Get one round of expert or tutor feedback on structure and argument — not last-minute proofreading the night before.

Week 4: Polish and protect the pass (Days 22–28)

  • Aim for 55–60% quality to create a safety margin. Grade boundaries and harsh second marking mean a “just pass” draft often slips to 38%.
  • Checklist: answered every part of the question, met learning outcomes, stayed within ±10% word count, referenced every claim, included introduction and conclusion, submitted in correct file format well before the deadline.
  • Submit early. A second late penalty on a capped resit is catastrophic — many universities deduct 5–10 marks per day and a fail means repeating the year.

If you are juggling work, resits in multiple modules, or English is not your first language, do not attempt this alone in a week. Targeted support with structure, sources and exam technique is often the difference between 38% and a comfortable pass.

What If a Capped 40% Kills My First or 2:1?

This is the most painful question, so let us be honest. One capped module hurts, but it rarely single-handedly destroys a classification — maths helps:

  • First year often does not count (or counts 0–10%) at most English universities. A Level 4 capped resit is a progression issue, not a classification disaster.
  • Later years are weighted. A common split is Year 2: Year 3 = 40:60 or 25:75. A 40% in one 20-credit module in Year 2 can be offset by 65%+ elsewhere.
  • Condonement and compensation may help. Some boards condone a narrow fail (e.g. 35–39%) if your overall average is strong, or compensate with credits. You cannot usually be condoned and resit for a higher mark — accepting condonement keeps the original mark.

Practical strategy if you are capped:

  1. Calculate your weighted average with 40% in the resit module. Identify how many marks you need across remaining modules to secure your target boundary.
  2. Prioritise high-credit modules (dissertation, final-year project) — a 65% in a 40-credit dissertation outweighs a 40% in a 20-credit resit.
  3. If illness genuinely caused the failure, pursue mitigation before the resit for an uncapped sit rather than hoping to “make it up later”.
  4. Talk to your personal tutor about progression rules, trailing credits, and whether a full-time repeat year for uncapped marks is ever worth the fee and time — usually only if multiple modules failed.

A capped pass still means you demonstrated the learning outcomes. Employers rarely see module marks; they see degree classification and skills. Focus forward.

Final Thoughts

A capped resit assessment feels punishing, but it is a second chance many students use successfully every summer.
