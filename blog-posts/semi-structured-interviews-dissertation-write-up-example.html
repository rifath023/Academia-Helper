How to Write Up Semi-Structured Interviews for Your Dissertation (Guide + Example Questions)

This semi-structured interviews dissertation write-up example gives you a complete 10-question guide with interview guide probes, neutral wording rules, pilot and transcription steps, and a copy-ready dependability paragraph for interviews.

semi-structured interviews dissertation interview guide probes dependability interviews neutral wording qualitative methodology

If you are doing qualitative interviews, examiners will judge you on one thing above all: can they see exactly what you asked, why you asked it, and how you kept it rigorous? This semi-structured interviews dissertation write-up example shows you how to do that — from building your guide to writing the methodology paragraph that proves dependability for interviews.

Semi-structured interviews sit in the sweet spot for most dissertations. They are focused enough to answer your research questions but flexible enough to follow unexpected insights. The problem is that many students describe them in one vague sentence — “semi-structured interviews were conducted” — and lose marks for transparency, ethics, and trustworthiness. This guide fixes that with a full template you can adapt, plus rules for neutral wording and interview guide probes drawn from Kvale and Brinkmann.

What you will get: a Kvale/Brinkmann guide structure, a 10-question template table with probes and purpose, a 5-probe bank, pilot + transcription + coding workflow, a copy-adapt dependability paragraph, an ethics checklist, and 5 FAQs.

Why Semi-Structured Interviews Suit Most Dissertations

Semi-structured interviews use a pre-planned interview guide with open-ended questions, but the interviewer can reorder questions, probe deeper, and follow up on new ideas. That balance is why supervisors recommend them for exploratory, experiential, and process questions — how people experience supervision, why nurses stay in a role, what barriers managers face when implementing policy.

For a deeper comparison of formats, see our semi-structured interview guide. In short:

Your methodology write-up must therefore justify why semi-structured and not the other two. A single sentence is enough: “A semi-structured format was chosen because the study required comparable coverage of three research objectives while allowing participants to elaborate on context-specific meanings (Kvale and Brinkmann, 2015).” That citation matters — examiners expect you to anchor guide design in the methods literature, not just preference.

The Kvale and Brinkmann Guide Structure: Intro, Grand-Tour, Probes, Closing

Kvale and Brinkmann (2015) argue that a good interview is crafted like a conversation with a purpose. Your guide should have four visible stages. When you describe this structure in your dissertation, you immediately signal methodological awareness.

1. Introduction and briefing (2–3 minutes)

Script this verbatim. State the purpose in plain language, how long it will take, that participation is voluntary, how recording and anonymity work, and that there are no right or wrong answers. Example script: “Thank you for taking part. I’m exploring how early-career teachers experience mentoring. This will take about 45 minutes. With your permission I’ll audio-record so I can transcribe accurately. You can skip any question or withdraw at any time without giving a reason. Everything will be anonymised with a pseudonym.” Including this script in your appendix is strong evidence of ethical consistency.

2. Grand-tour and experience questions (core of the guide)

Start broad and descriptive before moving to evaluative or sensitive topics. A grand-tour opener — “Walk me through a typical week in your current role” — settles participants and generates natural language you can probe later. Sequence from concrete experience to reflection to future-oriented questions. Keep each main question singular and jargon-free.

3. Planned probes and follow-ups

Probes are where semi-structured richness comes from. Write 2–3 interview guide probes under every main question in advance, so you do not freeze or lead under pressure. Distinguish between planned probes (in the guide) and emergent follow-ups (using the participant’s own words in the moment). Examiners want to see both.

4. Closing and debrief (3–5 minutes)

End with a catch-all — “Is there anything important about [topic] we haven’t covered that you’d like to add?” — then debrief: explain next steps, transcription, right to review, and who to contact. Thank the participant. This closing routinely produces the most reflective data of the whole interview, so never skip it.

Methodology tip: label these four stages as sub-headings in your appendix guide. An examiner can then see at a glance that your instrument follows Kvale and Brinkmann, not just a list of questions.

Note how each question does one job. That is deliberate. Double-barrelled questions — “How do you feel about supervision and what would you change?” — force participants to answer half the question and make coding a nightmare. If you catch yourself using “and” in a main question, split it.

Neutral Wording: Avoid Leading, Double-Barrelled and Loaded Questions

Neutral wording is the fastest way to strengthen credibility. Examiners routinely flag three errors:

  • Leading questions smuggle in your hypothesis. Poor: “So you found remote supervision isolating, didn’t you?” Better: “How did you experience remote supervision?” If you must check understanding, mirror their word: “You mentioned ‘isolating’ — can you say more about that?”
  • Double-barrelled questions ask two things at once. Poor: “How often do you meet your mentor and how useful is it?” Better: Ask frequency first, then usefulness as a separate question with a probe.
  • Loaded, jargon or assumed-knowledge wording confuses or steers. Poor: “How does your transformative pedagogical praxis manifest?” Better: “How would you describe your teaching approach in practice?” Pilot every term with a non-specialist.

Three practical rules solve most problems. First, start questions with what, how, describe, walk me through rather than why — “why” can sound interrogative early on; save it for gentle probes once rapport is built. Second, keep questions under 20 words and one clause. Third, replace evaluative adjectives (“effective,” “poor,” “successful”) with the participant’s own vocabulary wherever possible. Write the adjective-free version first, then add a neutral probe such as “How do you evaluate that?”

Document these choices in your methodology: “Questions were worded openly and neutrally, avoiding leading and double-barrelled formulations, and refined after pilot feedback (see Appendix C).” One sentence like that pre-empts a common viva question.

5-Probe Bank You Can Reuse in Any Interview

Memorise these five interview guide probes so you never lead when a participant gives a short answer. They work because they are content-free — they deepen without directing:

  1. Elaboration probe: “Can you say a little more about that?” — Use when an answer is brief or abstract. It signals interest without assuming meaning.
  2. Exemplification probe: “Can you give me a specific example of what that looked like?” — Moves from general opinion to concrete incident, which is far easier to code.
  3. Sequence probe: “What happened next? And then what?” — Reconstructs process and timeline without interpreting cause.
  4. Mirroring / echo probe: “You said ‘unsupported’ — what did ‘unsupported’ mean in that moment?” — Repeats their exact word back. This is the most powerful neutral technique for clarifying meaning.
  5. Silence + check probe: Pause 3–5 seconds, then “Is there anything else about that you think is important?” — Silence feels awkward but consistently yields the richest elaboration. Follow it with the closing catch-all at the end.

In your write-up, name your probe strategy explicitly: “Planned probes (elaboration, exemplification, and mirroring) were included in the guide, supplemented by emergent follow-ups grounded in participants’ own language.” That sentence shows you understand probing as a skill, not just extra questions.

Ethics and Consent: What to Write (and Attach)

Interview dissertations live or die on ethics transparency. At minimum, your methodology must state: institutional ethics approval (reference number and date), how participants were recruited and informed, that written informed consent was obtained, the right to withdraw, how anonymity and confidentiality are protected (pseudonyms, removal of identifiers, secure storage), and how data are stored under GDPR / institutional policy.

Example wording: “Ethical approval was granted by [University] Ethics Committee (Ref: XYZ, 12 March 2026). Participants received an information sheet and signed consent forms covering purpose, voluntary participation, recording, anonymisation, secure storage on an encrypted drive for [X years], and the right to withdraw up to [two weeks post-interview] without consequence.”

Practical tips that examiners notice: obtain consent for recording separately from consent to participate; confirm consent verbally on tape at the start; avoid interviewing direct reports or students you assess without a clear mitigation; and never promise absolute confidentiality where safeguarding disclosures may require action — promise anonymisation and explain limits. Attach your information sheet, consent form, and debrief sheet as appendices and cite them in-text.

Pilot, Transcription and Coding: Linking Fieldwork to Analysis

A two-interview pilot is the cheapest validity boost available. Pilot with people similar to your sample, time the interview, note which questions produce thin or confused answers, and revise wording. Log every change with a date — “Q4 split into two after Pilot 1 (12 April); jargon term ‘scaffolding’ replaced with ‘support’ after Pilot 2” — because that log becomes your dependability evidence.

For recording and transcription, state equipment (e.g., encrypted recorder + Teams backup), file naming, and transcription approach. Most dissertations use intelligent verbatim (word-for-word minus fillers) unless discourse analysis requires full Jefferson notation. Transcribe yourself for at least two interviews to stay close to the data, then check automated transcripts manually. Anonymise during transcription — replace names, places, and employers with [P03], [Hospital A] — and keep a separate key file.

Finally, link interviews to analysis explicitly. Name your method (most commonly Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis), describe familiarisation, initial coding, theme development, and how you used software or manual charts. Then point forward: “Transcripts were coded inductively in NVivo 14; the codebook and reflexive memos form part of the audit trail.” Our walkthrough on how to code interview transcripts gives you the step-by-step that follows naturally from this guide.

Dependability Paragraph Example (Copy and Adapt)

Dependability in interviews — Lincoln and Guba’s qualitative parallel to reliability — asks: could another researcher follow your decisions? You demonstrate it through an audit trail, not through statistics. For the full trustworthiness framework (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability), see trustworthiness in qualitative research.

Paste-adapt the paragraph below into your methodology chapter (around 120–150 words is ideal):

“Dependability was addressed through a transparent audit trail (Lincoln and Guba, 1985). The interview guide (Appendix C, v3, dated 20 April 2026) records all revisions following two pilot interviews, including rewording of Q5 for neutrality and splitting of the original Q7 to avoid a double-barrelled formulation. All interviews (n=12, 35–60 minutes) were audio-recorded, transcribed intelligent-verbatim within 72 hours, and anonymised with pseudonyms. Coding followed Braun and Clarke (2022): an initial codebook (Appendix D), reflexive memos after each interview, and fortnightly peer-debrief with the supervisor, whose feedback on theme boundaries is logged. Raw recordings, transcripts, codebook versions, and memos are retained on an encrypted drive to permit external audit.”

Why this works: it names the criterion, cites the source, lists concrete artefacts with locations and dates, quantifies the sample, and states retention. An examiner reading it knows exactly where to verify each claim. Adjust the numbers, dates, and appendix letters to match your dissertation — never submit the placeholder text verbatim.

Dissertation Checklist: Before You Submit

  • Guide covers each research objective / sub-question with 8–12 singular, open, neutrally worded questions?
  • Each main question has 2–3 planned probes plus space for emergent follow-ups in participants’ own words?
  • Intro script, grand-tour opener, and closing catch-all + debrief are scripted verbatim?
  • No leading, double-barrelled, jargon-heavy, or closed yes/no main questions (checked after pilot)?
  • Ethics approval ref, information sheet, consent form, withdrawal window, anonymisation and storage stated?
  • Pilot (n=2) logged with dated changes; guide version-controlled in appendix?
  • Recording, file naming, transcription convention (verbatim / intelligent verbatim) and anonymisation described?
  • Analysis method named and linked to transcripts; codebook + memos retained for audit?
  • Dependability paragraph written with citations (Lincoln and Guba; Kvale and Brinkmann; Braun and Clarke)?
  • Full guide + sample transcript excerpt included in appendices; full transcripts available on request per consent?

Frequently Asked Questions

For a 30–60 minute dissertation interview, aim for 8–12 main questions plus planned probes. Fewer than 6 rarely covers your research objectives; more than 12 usually rushes participants and produces thin data. Map one main question to one objective or sub-question, with 2–3 probes ready to deepen answers without leading.

State your design (semi-structured, mode, duration, setting), justify why it fits your research questions, describe your interview guide structure, sampling and recruitment, pilot, ethics and consent, recording and transcription, analysis method, and trustworthiness. Include your guide in an appendix and cite Kvale and Brinkmann (2015) and Lincoln and Guba (1985) for guide design and dependability.

Keep probes short, singular, and grounded in the participant’s own words.

Final Thoughts

A strong semi-structured interviews dissertation write-up example is not just a list of questions — it is proof of craft. When you show a Kvale-informed structure, neutral wording, purposeful interview guide probes, a logged pilot, careful transcription, and a concrete dependability trail, you turn “I did some interviews” into a defensible methodology chapter.

Start from the 10-question template above, pilot it twice, revise honestly, and write the dependability paragraph while the decisions are fresh — not the night before submission. Your future viva self will thank you.
