1. What Does Cronbach's Alpha Less Than 0.7 Actually Mean?

Cronbach's alpha measures internal consistency — whether the items in your scale hang together and measure the same underlying construct. It ranges from 0 to 1. Higher means respondents who score high on one item tend to score high on the others.

The famous 0.70 cut-off comes from Nunnally (1978) and Nunnally & Bernstein (1994), who suggested 0.70 as acceptable for early-stage research, 0.80 as good, and 0.90 as excellent for applied settings. George & Mallery (2003), cited in almost every SPSS textbook, gave the rule of thumb most supervisors quote:

Cronbach's AlphaInterpretationWhat it means for your dissertation
≥ .90ExcellentStrong reliability. Check for redundant items if > .95.
.80 – .89GoodSafe to proceed. Report and move on.
.70 – .79AcceptableMeets most university thresholds. No fix needed.
.60 – .69QuestionableOften accepted for short scales / exploratory work with justification.
.50 – .59PoorNeeds diagnosis. Try the 6 fixes below before reporting.
< .50UnacceptableDo not average items into a total score without fixing or re-theorising.

Three nuances students miss:

  • Alpha depends on number of items. A 3-item scale with α = .65 can be perfectly normal, while a 20-item scale with α = .65 signals real problems. Short scales are mathematically penalised — the Spearman-Brown principle. Cite this if your supervisor worries.
  • Alpha is not validity. A high alpha does not prove you measured the right construct, and a modest alpha does not prove your theory is wrong. It is only about consistency.
  • Some fields accept .60. In exploratory research, psychology pilot studies, and newly developed questionnaires, many journals accept α ≥ .60 (Hair et al., 2010; Pallant, 2020). Always check your university handbook and past dissertations in your department — that precedent matters more than a generic rule.
Bottom line: alpha < 0.7 is a yellow flag, not a red card. Diagnose first, then decide whether to fix, split, or honestly report.

2. Why Is Your Cronbach's Alpha Low? 5 Common Causes

Before you try to change alpha in SPSS, understand why it is low. The SPSS output rarely tells you the cause — you have to infer it. Here are the five culprits we see most in dissertation datasets:

Cause 1: Forgotten reverse-coding (the #1 student error)

Most Likert scales mix positively and negatively worded items to catch acquiescence bias. Example: "I feel confident using SPSS" (positive) alongside "I feel anxious about statistics" (negative). If you average them without reversing the negative item, high confidence (5) plus high anxiety (5) cancel out, correlations turn negative, and alpha collapses — sometimes even below zero. If your Inter-Item Correlation Matrix shows negative values, this is almost certainly the cause.

Cause 2: One or two bad items

A single poorly worded, ambiguous, double-barrelled, or off-topic item can drag a whole scale down. Classic signs: its Corrected Item-Total Correlation is below .30 (often near zero or negative) and the Alpha if Item Deleted column shows alpha would jump if you removed it. This is the easiest legitimate fix.

Cause 3: Too few items

Alpha rewards longer scales. A 3-item job-satisfaction scale will almost always score lower than a 10-item version, even if the items are good. If your scale has fewer than 5 items and alpha is .60–.69, report the mean inter-item correlation as well — Briggs & Cheek (1986) recommend .20–.40 as acceptable for short scales. Examiners respect this.

Cause 4: Multidimensionality — you lumped two constructs together

Alpha assumes unidimensionality. If your 8 "motivation" items actually measure 4 intrinsic-motivation items and 4 extrinsic-motivation items, overall alpha will be low even though each subscale is reliable. Solution: split and test subscales separately. Never average apples and oranges into one total.

Cause 5: Data quality problems

Straight-liners (respondents ticking all 3s), miscoded missing values (e.g., 99 left as a real score), tiny samples (n < 50), or mixing very heterogeneous groups can all depress alpha. This is why cleaning survey data in SPSS before reliability testing is non-negotiable.

3. First: Did You Run the Reliability Test Correctly?

About a third of "low alpha" panics we see are just wrong SPSS settings. Re-run it properly once — it takes 60 seconds:

  1. Go to Analyze > Scale > Reliability Analysis.
  2. Move only the items from one scale / subscale into the Items box. Do not put your entire questionnaire in at once.
  3. Set Model: Alpha.
  4. Click Statistics and tick: Item, Scale, Scale if item deleted. Under Inter-Item, tick Correlations. Click Continue.
  5. Click OK. Read three tables: Reliability Statistics (overall alpha), Item-Total Statistics (the diagnostic gold), and Inter-Item Correlation Matrix.

If you selected the wrong items, forgot Statistics options, or included a demographic variable (age, gender) in the scale, your alpha is meaningless. Fix that first, then move to the six fixes.

Corrected Item-Total CorrelationAll ≥ .30, ideally ≥ .40Any < .30, zero, or negative
Alpha if Item DeletedAll lower than overall alphaOne value clearly higher than overall alpha
Inter-Item CorrelationsMostly .20 – .70, all positiveNegatives or many < .15
Number of items (N of Items)Matches your codebookWrong count = wrong items entered

4. Six Legitimate Fixes to Raise Alpha in SPSS

These are in the order you should try them. Do them one at a time and re-run alpha after each — do not do all six at once or you will not know what worked.

Fix 1 — Reverse-score negatively worded items (biggest win)

When to use: any negative inter-item correlations, or your questionnaire has items marked (R) such as "I feel *un*prepared".

SPSS clicks: Transform > Recode into Different Variables → move the negatively worded item across → name it e.g. Q3R → click Change → Old and New Values → for a 1–5 scale enter 1 → 5, 2 → 4, 3 → 3, 4 → 2, 5 → 1 → Continue → OK. Repeat for each reversed item. Then re-run Reliability using the new R variables, not the originals.

Why it works: reversing aligns all items in the same direction so high scores consistently mean "more of the construct". We have seen alpha jump from .41 to .79 from this single step. Always document: "Items 3 and 7 were reverse-scored prior to analysis."

Fix 2 — Drop (or revise) items with Corrected Item-Total below .30

When to use: one or two items stand out in the Item-Total Statistics table.

SPSS clicks: open the Item-Total Statistics table → find the Corrected Item-Total Correlation column → flag any item < .30 → check its Alpha if Item Deleted value. If that value is higher than your overall alpha, that item is hurting reliability.

Example: overall α = .62. Q5 has item-total = .11 and Alpha if Deleted = .74. Removing Q5 is statistically justified. Re-run Reliability without Q5 and report both values: "Initial α = .62; after removal of Q5 (item-total r = .11), α = .74."

Important rule: need both statistics and theory. Ask: is Q5 badly worded, double-barrelled ("My lecturer is clear and inspiring"), or measuring something else? If yes, drop it. If the item is theoretically central, keep it and report the lower alpha as a limitation instead. Never delete half your scale just to chase .70 — examiners will notice and penalise loss of content validity.

ItemCorrected Item-TotalAlpha if Deleted (overall α=.62)Decision
Q1.52.55Keep — strong contributor
Q2.48.57Keep
Q3R (reversed).45.58Keep — reversal worked
Q4.39.59Keep — above .30
Q5.11.74Drop — low item-total, alpha jumps

Fix 3 — Inspect the Inter-Item Correlation Matrix

SPSS clicks: same Reliability → Statistics → tick Correlations under Inter-Item → look at the matrix in output.

You want most values between .20 and .70. Values above .80 suggest redundancy (two items asking the same thing — consider dropping one). Values below .15 or negative suggest the item does not belong. If one item correlates poorly with all others, that confirms Fix 2. If many correlations are near zero, your scale is likely multidimensional — go to Fix 4.

Fix 4 — Split multidimensional scales into subscales

When to use: your scale mixes sub-topics, or a factor analysis / common sense suggests two constructs.

Example: an 8-item "Study Motivation" scale with 4 intrinsic items (interest, curiosity) and 4 extrinsic items (grades, parental pressure). Overall α = .58 looks terrible. Run Reliability separately: intrinsic α = .81, extrinsic α = .78. Both are good — the problem was lumping, not bad items.

SPSS clicks: run Analyze > Scale > Reliability Analysis twice, once per subscale. Report each alpha separately and analyse subscale totals separately in all later tests. State clearly which items belong to which subscale and cite the original scale author's structure.

Fix 5 — Clean data errors that silently kill alpha

SPSS clicks: Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Descriptives → check Minimum/Maximum for out-of-range values (e.g., 99, 0 on a 1–5 scale). Then Transform > Recode into Same Variables or set them as missing via Missing Values in Variable View. Sort Data View by each item to spot straight-liners (e.g., 5,5,5,5,5 across 20 items in 30 seconds) and consider excluding careless responders — document your rule and count.

Also check sample size. With n < 30, alpha is unstable — one odd respondent swings it wildly. If you have just collected a pilot (n = 20), a low alpha is expected; report it as pilot feedback and proceed to main data collection. For guidance see How to Clean Survey Data in SPSS.

Fix 6 — Decide: add items (future) vs. accept and report (now)

You cannot ethically add items after data collection just to push alpha up, and you must never duplicate items or fabricate responses. But you can note for your limitations that a longer validated version of the scale would likely improve reliability, citing the Spearman-Brown prophecy. If your scale has only 3–4 items and alpha is .65 with healthy item-totals (all > .30), the honest move is to keep the scale, report mean inter-item correlation (.20–.40), and proceed — most examiners accept this with a one-sentence justification.

✅ Ethical checklist for changing alpha: reverse-coding errors? Yes, always fix. Dropping 1–2 statistically + theoretically weak items? Yes, with transparency. Splitting subscales? Yes. Deleting items until alpha hits .70 with no justification, adding fake items, or running reliability 20 times and reporting only the best? No — that is p-hacking and examiners check.

5. What If Alpha Is Still Below 0.7 After All Fixes?

Sometimes you do everything right and alpha stays at .64 or .59. This happens with short, newly translated, or culturally adapted scales — and it is not an automatic fail. What examiners penalise is not low alpha, but hiding it or misusing the total score. Here is what to do:

  1. Report it transparently. State the exact alpha, number of items, and sample size for every scale. Report both original and revised alphas if you dropped an item. Never round .63 up to .7 or omit the reliability table.
  2. Add a one-paragraph limitation. Acknowledge the marginal reliability, suggest why (e.g., "three-item scale", "translated instrument", "heterogeneous student sample"), and note caution in interpreting correlations involving that variable. Examiners reward this honesty.
  3. Consider alternatives. For 2-item scales, report the Spearman-Brown coefficient or inter-item correlation instead of alpha (Eisinga et al., 2013). For multidimensional data, report McDonald's omega or split-half as a supplement — but only if you understand them; a clean alpha + honest limitation beats a misused omega.
  4. Do not average multidimensional items. If alpha stays below .50 and item-totals are all poor, do not compute a total score. Analyse items individually or re-theorise your construct, and discuss this with your supervisor before running regressions or t-tests — unreliable composites attenuate every downstream effect.

Useful citations to defend a .60–.69 alpha: Nunnally & Bernstein (1994) on exploratory thresholds, Hair et al. (2010) on .60 as acceptable in exploratory research, Pallant (2020) SPSS Survival Manual on short scales, and Briggs & Cheek (1986) on mean inter-item correlation of .20–.40. One sentence with two citations is enough — do not write a whole page apologising.

⛔ Never do this to raise alpha: copy-paste rows to inflate N, delete respondents because they lower alpha, duplicate an item under a new name, or report only the highest alpha from 15 unreported re-runs. All of these are academic misconduct and easily spotted from your .sav file and output timestamps.

6. Example APA Paragraph (Copy-Adapt for Your Dissertation)

Examiners want one clean paragraph per scale in your Methods / Results chapter. Adapt this template — it covers both the fixed and the still-low scenario, and follows APA 7th reporting:

"Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha in SPSS (Analyze > Scale > Reliability Analysis). The 5-item academic self-efficacy scale initially showed questionable reliability (α = .62). Inspection of Corrected Item-Total Correlations revealed one weak item (Q5, r = .11); Alpha if Item Deleted indicated reliability would improve to .74 without it. As Q5 was double-barrelled ('My lecturers are clear and supportive') and conceptually distinct, it was removed. The revised 4-item scale demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = .74, M = 3.82, SD = 0.61, n = 142). The 3-item perceived stress scale showed marginal reliability (α = .66); given the small number of items, mean inter-item correlation was also inspected (r = .32, within the recommended .20–.40 range; Briggs & Cheek, 1986) and the scale was retained with caution noted as a limitation."

7. Low-Alpha Checklist Before You Submit

Run through this in 10 minutes before sending your chapter to your supervisor:

  • ☐ Ran Reliability per subscale, not whole questionnaire at once?
  • ☐ Ticked Item / Scale / Scale if item deleted / Inter-Item Correlations?
  • ☐ All reverse-worded items recoded to new R variables and originals excluded?
  • ☐ Checked Corrected Item-Total ≥ .30 and Alpha if Deleted for every item?
  • ☐ Any dropped item has both statistical and theoretical justification, documented?
  • ☐ Reported both original and final alpha + k + n + M + SD?
  • ☐ Short scales (k < 5) also report mean inter-item r (.20–.40)?
  • ☐ Low alpha acknowledged in limitations with 1–2 citations?
  • ☐ Total scores only computed for sufficiently unidimensional, reliable scales?
  • ☐ Saved dated SPSS output (.spv) and syntax so you can prove every step?

Final Thoughts

A Cronbach's alpha less than 0.7 in SPSS feels scary at 2 a.m. before a deadline, but it is one of the most fixable problems in dissertation statistics. In order: re-run the reliability test in SPSS correctly, fix reverse coding, use the item-total and alpha-if-deleted columns to judge weak items, check inter-item correlations, split multidimensional scales, and clean your data. Most students find their alpha was never truly "low" — it was just mis-scored.

And if your alpha stays at .64 after honest fixes, you can still pass — and pass well — by reporting it transparently, citing thresholds for short and exploratory scales, and noting it as a limitation. Examiners fail hidden or manipulated reliability, not honest marginal reliability. Save your syntax, keep your output, and write the one clean APA paragraph that shows you understood what you did.
