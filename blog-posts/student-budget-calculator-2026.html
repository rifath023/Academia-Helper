Student Budget Calculator 2026: Monthly Spend Without Rent Explained

Rent gets all the attention, but non-rent spending decides whether your budget survives. We break down the average UK student monthly spend without rent — groceries, transport, bills and social — plus how part-time work fills the gap.

student budget calculator uk student budget student monthly spend student groceries cost part-time work students

Most budget guides start with rent — but monthly spend without rent is where student budgets actually succeed or fail. Two students can pay identical rent and finish the month £300 apart, purely because of groceries, transport, takeaways and nights out.

This student budget calculator for 2026 explains what UK students really spend once rent is removed, line by line, with low, average and high scenarios. We also cover grocery and transport hacks and the part-time work maths that closes the gap — so you can build a budget in 20 minutes that lasts all term.

Headline: What Do Students Spend Per Month Without Rent?

Across Save the Student, NatWest and ONS-aligned surveys, a realistic UK student monthly spend without rent in 2026 is £380–£550 for most students outside London, and £480–£680 in London. Frugal cooks can hit £280–£350; heavy takeaway and taxi users easily exceed £700.

  • Low (frugal, mostly cooking, cycling/walking): £280–£380
  • Average (mix of cooking + some eating out, bus pass): £380–£550 (£480–£680 London)
  • High (regular Deliveroo, Ubers, nights out): £600–£800+

Add rent (£550–£750 regions, £850–£1,200 London) and you return to the familiar £950–£1,250 / £1,300–£1,750 all-in totals — which is why non-rent spend deserves its own calculator.

Groceries: The £180–£260 Question

Food is the largest non-rent cost and the easiest to control. The average student grocery bill sits around £180–£220 per month when cooking most meals, rising to £260+ with regular meal deals (£3.75–£4.50 each add up to £80+ monthly if daily).

  • Shop the discounters first: Aldi and Lidl are 20–30% cheaper than Tesco/Sainsbury's for staples; Asian and Eastern European shops beat all supermarkets on rice, spices, lentils and fresh herbs.
  • Frozen is your friend: frozen veg, berries, fish and bread cut waste to near zero — UK students waste an estimated £15–£25 of food monthly.
  • Two meal-preps per week: chilli, curry, pasta bake and stir-fry at £1–£1.50 per portion vs £7–£12 takeaway. Batch-cooking twice weekly saves roughly £80–£120 monthly.
  • Yellow-sticker + apps: evening reductions plus Too Good To Go and Olio routinely deliver £15+ bags for £3–£5.
Sample £45 weekly shop: oats + milk (£4), rice/pasta (£4), chicken + mince (£10), frozen veg + tinned tomatoes/beans (£7), fruit (£5), bread/eggs/cheese (£8), snacks/coffee (£7). That is ~£195 per month and covers 18–20 home-cooked dinners.

Transport: Passes Beat Pay-As-You-Go

Transport averages £40–£70 monthly outside London but is the most variable line: walkers pay £0, while unmanaged train + Uber use can hit £150+.

  • Bus annual pass: £300–£500 per year (£25–£42/month) in most university cities — half the cost of daily singles.
  • 16–25 Railcard (£30/year): saves a third on rail; pays for itself in two long trips. Pair with split-ticketing apps.
  • London: 18+ Student Oyster + weekly capping keeps Zone 1–2 around £28–£35/week; cycling (Santander or own bike) can halve this.
  • Kill the £12 taxi habit: two Ubers per week = ~£100/month — more than a bus pass. Night buses, walking groups and pre-booked lifts save most of it.

Bills, Phone and Hidden Costs

  • Shared-house bills: £60–£90 per person monthly (gas/electric/water/broadband + TV licence split). Fix unit rates when signing; submit meter readings monthly to avoid catch-up bills.
  • Phone: £8–£15 SIM-only (Lebara, Voxi, Smarty, Giffgaff) beats £25+ contracts. Most students need 20–30GB, not unlimited.
  • Laundry + toiletries + printing: £30–£50 combined. £3.50 wash + £2.50 dry twice weekly = ~£48/month — drying racks and a shared wash rota halve it.
  • Course extras: lab coats, DBS checks, field trips, society memberships (£5–£50 each). Budget £150 per year and you will rarely be surprised.

Part-Time Work: The Maths That Closes the Gap

The maximum English maintenance loan (~£10,227/year outside London ≈ £850/month over 12 months, or ~£1,136 over a 9-month term) rarely covers the £950–£1,250 all-in spend. The shortfall of £100–£400 monthly is why most students work.

  • At National Minimum Wage (21–22: £12.21/hr from April 2025; 18–20: £10/hr): one 8-hour weekend shift ≈ £80–£98 after minimal tax — roughly £320–£390/month. That alone closes the typical gap.
  • Best-paying student roles: campus ambassador (£12–£15/hr), tutoring (£20–£40/hr), hospitality + tips, night-shift retail premiums, and paid research participation (£10–£20/hr).
  • Cap hours to protect grades: Save the Student links long hours to the 33% grade-impact figure. Stay at 10–15 hrs in term, save full-time hours for holidays (a 37.5-hr summer week at £12.21 = ~£458 before tax).
  • Use university help first: bursaries (£500–£3,000/year), hardship funds and food pantries are grants, not loans — apply before taking extra shifts in exam season.

Your 20-Minute Budget Calculator

  • Step 1: income per month = (loan instalment ÷ months) + grant + family + (contracted shifts × hourly pay). Do not count overtime.
  • Step 2: subtract rent first. Remainder = your non-rent budget. If remainder is under £380 (regions) or £480 (London), you need cheaper housing or guaranteed work — not hope.
  • Step 3: allocate remainder: groceries 45%, transport 12%, bills/phone 18%, social 15%, buffer 10%.
  • Step 4: track weekly, not monthly. £110/week non-rent is easier to feel than £475/month.
  • Step 5: automate a £20/week transfer to savings on payday — you will build a £500 emergency buffer in six months.

London vs Regions and International Students

Location changes everything. A student in Sheffield, Cardiff or Newcastle can live comfortably on a non-rent budget of £380–£450 per month, while the same lifestyle in London costs £500–£650 because of higher grocery prices, Zone 1–2 transport and social costs. The NatWest Student Living Index consistently shows this gap: lower rents plus lower daily spend make cities like Leicester, Derby and Belfast far more forgiving when loans fall short.

  • Regionally: walk or cycle, use the £300–£500 annual bus pass, and shop at Aldi, Lidl and local markets. Many northern cities have low-cost student nights and free campus societies that keep social spend under £60 monthly.
  • In London: get the 18+ Student Oyster, cook with friends in bulk, and use free museums, parks and society events instead of £15–£20 paid outings. Even two fewer takeaways per week saves £60–£80 monthly.
  • International students: add £50–£100 monthly for visa-related costs averaged across the year (health surcharge, visa fees, flights home, higher deposits). Check whether your visa limits term-time work to 20 hours — most students should still cap at 10–15 to protect grades.

Wherever you study, the rule holds: pick housing first, then force non-rent spend to fit what is left — not the other way round.

Final Thoughts

Once rent is removed, the average student budget is not mysterious: £380–£550 outside London, £480–£680 in London, driven by groceries, transport and takeaways. Control food, buy the pass, cap taxis, and let one sensible weekend shift — not five exhausting ones — close the gap. That is the whole calculator.
