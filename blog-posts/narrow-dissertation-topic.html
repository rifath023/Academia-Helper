    Is Your Dissertation Topic Too Broad? 7 Ways to Narrow It

    Seven practical ways to reduce an overbroad dissertation topic without making the study trivial.

    narrow dissertation topicresearch scoperesearch questiondissertation topicresearch planning

    Quick Answer

    How do you narrow a broad dissertation topic?

    Reduce one or more dimensions of the study: the population, location, time period, industry or context, variables, theoretical lens, outcome or research method. A good dissertation question is narrow enough to answer with the time, data and word count available.

    Signs Your Topic Is Too Broad

    A broad topic often contains several populations, outcomes, contexts or large concepts without clear boundaries. If your literature search returns huge numbers of unrelated results, your objectives resemble separate studies or your questionnaire needs many disconnected sections, the scope is probably too wide.

    Another warning sign is a title that could describe a textbook chapter, such as “The Impact of Technology on Education.”

    1. Narrow the Population

    Replace “university students” with a defined group such as final-year business students, international postgraduate students or students using a particular learning format. A clearer population makes sampling and interpretation easier.

    Choose the group because it fits the research question, not only because it is convenient. Explain why that population is analytically relevant.

    2. Limit the Location or Context

    Move from a global topic to one country, university, organisation, sector or platform when that setting matters. “Employee engagement in the banking sector in one country” is more manageable than “employee engagement worldwide.”

    Do not add a place name only to make the title look specific. Explain why the context matters to the problem.

    3. Define a Time Period

    If the topic involves policy change, markets, technology or historical development, a clear time window can make the research analysable. Compare a defined period rather than discussing a decade of unrelated change.

    Use a period that matches the data you can actually obtain and the event or process you want to examine.

    4. Reduce the Number of Variables

    A student model with many predictors becomes difficult to justify, measure and analyse. Keep the variables most strongly supported by theory and most relevant to the research question.

    Removing a weak variable can reduce questionnaire length, literature burden, multicollinearity and analysis complexity without reducing the quality of the central question.

    5. Choose One Main Outcome

    Instead of studying satisfaction, loyalty, performance, motivation and retention in one dissertation, choose the outcome that best matches the research problem. Secondary outcomes can be removed or reserved for future research.

    One clear dependent variable often creates a stronger conceptual framework and discussion.

    6. Use a Specific Theoretical or Analytical Lens

    A theory or model can narrow which relationships matter. Rather than covering every possible explanation for entrepreneurship intention, a study might focus on self-efficacy and perceived risk within a defined framework.

    The lens should organise the study, not force evidence into a theory that does not fit the context.

    7. Limit What the Method Must Answer

    A dissertation does not need to answer every question using surveys, interviews and secondary data together. Choose the method that best addresses the main question and can be completed well.

    Mixed methods can be valuable, but only when both components have a clear purpose and enough time is available for collection and integration.

    Use the One-Sentence Scope Test

    Try to complete this sentence: “This study examines [relationship or experience] among [population] in [context] during [period], using [method].” If it needs several semicolons and multiple outcomes, the scope may still be too broad.

    Then check whether the title, objectives, framework and data collection all match that sentence.

    Write the original topic in one line and the revised question beneath it, then identify exactly what was removed: a population, variable, location, outcome, method or time period. If you cannot point to a concrete boundary that changed, the topic may still be broad even if the wording sounds more specific.

    Write the Broad and Narrow Versions Side by Side

    Before you move on, compare the revised plan with the dissertation handbook, assessment criteria and latest supervisor guidance. Check that the research question, objectives, method, evidence and remaining timeline still point in the same direction. A short consistency check now can prevent repeated corrections later, especially when the project has changed under time pressure.

    Final Consistency Check

    After reducing scope, make sure the question still allows explanation, comparison, evaluation or interpretation. A topic can become so narrow that it only asks for a simple description. The aim is not the smallest possible project; it is a focused project with enough evidence and conceptual depth to support a meaningful dissertation argument within the available time.

    Key Takeaways

    • Narrow population, context, time, variables or outcomes.
    • Use theory to focus the relationships that matter.
    • Choose one main research problem.
    • Make data collection match the narrowed scope.
    • Check feasibility against time, access and word count.
    • Update the title, objectives, framework and method together.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How do I know if my dissertation topic is too broad?
    If it contains many populations, outcomes, variables or contexts and cannot be answered within the available data and word count, it is probably too broad.
    Can I narrow my topic after proposal approval?
    Often yes, but check whether supervisor, proposal or ethics approval needs to be updated.
    Should I narrow by population or variables first?
    Start with whichever dimension creates the biggest feasibility problem. Population and variable count are often practical places to reduce scope.
    Can a dissertation topic be too narrow?
    Yes. It should still support meaningful analysis and enough evidence to answer a research question.
    Does a case study help narrow scope?
    It can because a defined organisation, place or case creates boundaries, but the case still needs a clear analytical question.
    Should the title change when I narrow the topic?
    Yes if the old title no longer accurately describes the population, variables or context.
