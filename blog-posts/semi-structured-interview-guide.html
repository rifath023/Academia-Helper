    How to Create a Semi-Structured Interview Guide for Research

    A practical method for designing open, flexible interview questions that stay aligned with your dissertation research questions while leaving room for participant experiences.

    semi structured interview guidequalitative interviewdissertation interviewsresearch questionsinterview probes

    Quick Answer

    How do you create a semi-structured interview guide?

    Start from the research questions, identify the main topics you need to explore, write open and neutral questions for each topic, add optional probes, arrange the questions from easy to more sensitive areas, include an opening and closing, then pilot the guide and revise unclear or leading questions.

    What Is a Semi-Structured Interview Guide?

    A semi-structured interview guide is a planned list of topics, core questions and possible probes used to support a flexible qualitative interview. Unlike a rigid questionnaire, it gives the interviewer room to change the order, ask follow-up questions and explore unexpected but relevant experiences.

    The guide should create consistency across interviews without making every conversation sound identical. Its purpose is to help you collect data that can answer the research question while allowing participants to explain what matters from their own perspective.

    Start With the Research Questions

    Write the main research question at the top of your planning sheet. Then break it into the areas you need participants to discuss. For a study about students' engagement with online learning, possible areas might include routines, motivation, interaction with lecturers, technology, distractions and support.

    Do not simply read the formal research question aloud to participants. Convert each area into everyday language. The interview should sound like a conversation, not a methodology chapter.

    Plan for Sensitive Questions and Ethical Boundaries

    Place potentially sensitive topics after easier questions, once some rapport has developed. Use non-judgemental wording and remind participants that they can decline to answer where your consent process provides that choice. Do not pressure someone to disclose information that is not necessary for the research question.

    Before data collection, make sure the guide matches the ethics approval, participant information sheet and consent process used by your institution. If you substantially change the topics after approval, check whether an amendment is required.

    Pilot the Interview Guide Before the Main Study

    A pilot interview can reveal questions that sound clear on paper but confuse participants in conversation. Check whether the questions produce detailed answers, whether the order feels natural and whether the interview can be completed within the planned time.

    After piloting, remove repeated questions, simplify academic wording and add probes where answers were too general. Keep a clean final version and record any approved changes made during the study.

    Common Interview Guide Mistakes

    • Using academic jargon that participants would not normally use.
    • Writing leading questions that assume a problem or preferred answer.
    • Asking double-barrelled questions about several issues at once.
    • Creating so many questions that there is no time for follow-up.
    • Starting with highly sensitive questions before rapport is established.
    • Treating the guide as a script that cannot change during the conversation.

    A strong semi-structured guide gives direction without closing down the participant's story. The best questions are purposeful, understandable and flexible enough to generate detailed examples.

    Key Takeaways

    • Map every main interview topic back to the research question.
    • Use open, neutral questions written in everyday language.
    • Prepare optional probes to deepen important answers.
    • Move from easy descriptive questions toward more sensitive topics.
    • Pilot the guide for clarity, timing and flow.
    • Keep the final guide aligned with your ethics approval and consent materials.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How many questions should a semi-structured interview have?
    There is no fixed number. Use enough core questions to cover the research aims while leaving time for follow-up. A shorter guide with good probes often produces richer data than a long questionnaire-style list.
    Can I change the order of semi-structured interview questions?
    Yes. Flexibility is a defining feature of semi-structured interviewing. You can follow the participant's story while still ensuring the main topics are covered.
    What is a probe in a qualitative interview?
    A probe is a follow-up prompt used to clarify, deepen or expand an answer, such as asking for an example, explanation, feeling or description of what happened next.
    Should interview questions match the research questions exactly?
    They should be aligned, but they do not need identical wording. Research questions are written for the study; interview questions should be understandable and natural for participants.
    Should I pilot my interview guide?
    Yes, where practical. Piloting helps you identify confusing wording, poor sequencing, timing problems and topics that do not generate useful data.
    Can I add new questions during data collection?
    Sometimes, but changes should remain relevant to the research aims and consistent with your ethics approval. Document meaningful changes and follow your institution's requirements.
