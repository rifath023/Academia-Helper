Health Becomes Australia's Number One Degree as IT Falls (2026)

Health has overtaken every other field as Australia's top degree choice while IT slides. Here is what the ABC January 2026 data means for your course decision.

health degree number one degree nursing Australia IT decline course choice

A changing of the guard just happened in Australian higher education: Health is now the number one field of study, while IT has fallen down the preference rankings — a shift reported by the ABC in January 2026 that confirms what admissions offices had sensed for two years. After a decade in which coding bootcamps and six-figure tech salaries dominated career advice, students are voting for stethoscopes over software: secure work, visible social purpose and burnout-proof demand.

This guide unpacks the Health number one degree story: what the ABC (2026) data shows, why IT fell, which health courses lead on employment, and how to choose between the two fields without chasing yesterday's headlines. Whether you are a school-leaver, a career-switcher or a parent advising one, these are the numbers that matter.

What the ABC (January 2026) Reported

The ABC's January 2026 analysis of university preference and enrolment data found Health disciplines topping the field-of-study rankings, ahead of business, education and society-and-culture groupings, with information technology sliding from its pandemic-era peak. Nursing led within health, followed by allied-health courses — physiotherapy, occupational therapy, psychology pathways — plus public health and paramedicine. The shift was broad-based across states and ATAR bands, not a single-city artefact.

Context matters: preferences are not enrolments, and first-preference counts move faster than final student loads. But directionally the signal is strong and corroborated by UAC (2026) application data showing health applications growing while some computing courses softened. Universities have responded by expanding clinical placement capacity — the binding constraint on health growth — and trimming or refocusing lower-demand IT offerings toward cyber security, data and AI specialisations where employer demand stayed firm.

Why Health Rose to Number One

Four forces lifted health. First, workforce shortages made demand visible: aged care, disability services under the NDIS, mental health and regional hospitals all advertise chronic vacancies, and students notice. Second, employment certainty: health graduates post 85-92% short-term employment, the strongest band nationally, with structured graduate years replacing the dreaded experience paradox. Third, purpose: post-pandemic cohorts consistently rank meaningful work above maximum pay, and few fields match health for visible human impact. Fourth, migration pathways: health occupations dominate skills-shortage lists, giving international students clearer post-study prospects than many generalist alternatives.

Demographics lock the trend in. Australia's ageing population guarantees rising demand for nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists and aged-care professionals for decades — demand that cannot be offshored or automated away. Government funding follows the same logic: clinical training places, rural incentives and graduate-year programs all tilt the economics toward health in ways no marketing campaign could manufacture.

Why IT Fell — and Why the Obituaries Are Wrong

IT's slide has real causes. The 2023-2024 tech correction ended the era of mass graduate intake at global firms, AI-coding headlines spooked school-leavers into assuming junior developers were finished, and starting-salary growth flattened just as health's security story peaked. Reported preferences fell accordingly — but preferences are sentiment, and sentiment overshoots.

The obituaries are wrong for three reasons. Graduate Outcomes Survey patterns still show computing employment in the 72-80% band with above-median starting salaries for skilled graduates. Cyber security, data engineering and AI-adjacent roles remain shortage occupations with employers complaining of thin applicant pools. And AI tooling has arguably raised the value of credentialled problem-solvers: employers want graduates who can direct, verify and secure AI-assisted systems, not fewer technologists. IT did not die; the bar rose, and casual interest fell away — which is good news for serious applicants facing thinner competition.

Choosing Between Health and IT Without Regret

Run a fit test before a trend test. Health suits students who like people-facing work, cope with bodily realities and shift rosters, and want structured progression from graduate year to specialisation. IT suits builders who enjoy abstract problem-solving, self-directed portfolio work and fast-changing toolchains. Trial both cheaply: a first-aid certificate plus aged-care volunteering reveals your stomach for care work in a month; a free programming course plus one shipped project reveals your appetite for code just as fast.

Hybrid paths are underrated. Health informatics, bioinformatics, medical imaging technology and digital-health product roles combine both trends — and employers in these niches report some of the thinnest talent pipelines nationally. A nursing degree followed by digital-health postgraduate study, or a computing degree aimed at health-sector employers, can capture the number-one field's demand with the fallen field's pay flexibility.

Applying Smart in a Health-Surge Year

  • Apply across the health spectrum: pair aspirational physiotherapy or pharmacy preferences with nursing or allied-health assisting pathways at lower ATARs.
  • Front-load experience evidence: care volunteering, first-aid certification and shadowing days strengthen applications and confirm fit.
  • Check placement capacity: ask faculties about clinical-hour guarantees and rural placement support before preferencing — popular courses strain placement supply.
  • For IT applicants: specialise early in cyber, data or AI-adjacent streams, build a public portfolio from first year, and chase internships ruthlessly — the bar is higher but the field is emptier.
  • Write standout personal statements: health and IT both increasingly weigh written motivation evidence; generic statements sink in competitive rounds.

ATAR Cut-Offs, Pathways and Second Chances

The health surge has a sharp edge: popular courses now carry demanding entry thresholds. Recent UAC (2026) cut-off patterns show nursing commonly requiring ATARs in the 70s to low 80s, paramedicine and occupational therapy in the mid-80s to 90s, physiotherapy and pharmacy in the high 80s to mid-90s, and undergraduate medicine above 95 plus UCAT performance. Missing a cut-off is not the end of the story, but it demands a pathway plan rather than wishful thinking. Diploma-to-degree packaged offers, regional-campus entry with lower thresholds, and generalist-science first years with internal transfer routes all convert near-misses into eventual places — provided students check credit-transfer rules before enrolling.

For IT, the softer demand cycle is a strategic opening. Cut-offs have eased in several generalist computing degrees while cyber security and data specialisations hold firmer, meaning well-prepared applicants face genuinely improved odds. Mature-age and VET pathways have also widened: Certificate IV and diploma holders with strong portfolios now enter many technology degrees with credit, shortening the course and its living-cost burden. Whatever your ATAR, the rule holds — preference every viable pathway in order, accept the best offer received, and transfer upward on results rather than sitting out a year that costs both time and money.

Final Thoughts

Health as the number one degree reflects rational student arithmetic — secure, purposeful, well-supported work — while IT's fall reflects sentiment overshooting a real but narrow correction. Choose by fit, trial both fields cheaply, and consider hybrid paths that capture both trends.
